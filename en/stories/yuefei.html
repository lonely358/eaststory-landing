The Death of Yue Fei: The General Who Won His Battles and Came Home to a Prison Cell

War & Upheaval · Nov 20, 2026 · ~13 min read

In 1140, Yue Fei led his army north, won two great victories at Yancheng and Yingchang, and advanced to Zhuxianzhen, one step from recovering Kaifeng. At exactly that moment, the court's gold plaques arrived at the front one after another, ordering him to withdraw. Less than a year after Yue Fei returned to court, he was in the prison of the Court of Judicial Review. On January 27, 1142 — New Year's Eve — he was granted death in that prison in Hangzhou, at thirty-nine years of age by the traditional count. His eldest son Yue Yun and his general Zhang Xian were beheaded the same day. Twenty years later the court rehabilitated him, restored his offices, and reburied him by West Lake. When Han Shizhong asked Qin Hui to his face what Yue Fei's crime was, Qin Hui answered with three words: "Mo xu you" — "perhaps there is."

I. A Farmer's Son from Tangyin

Yue Fei was born on March 24, 1103, into a farming household in Tangyin, Xiangzhou — Tangyin is in present-day Anyang, Henan — and his courtesy name was Pengju. His family had farmed for generations, and life was tight. According to his grandson Yue Ke's account, Yue Fei loved learning from childhood and knew the Zuozhuan and the military classics of Sun Tzu and Wu Qi by heart; when the family had no books he borrowed them wherever he could and copied them out to read. His maternal grandfather hired a man to teach him archery; the teacher, Zhou Tong, thought so highly of him that he taught him all his art of the bow. After Zhou Tong died, Yue Fei went to his grave on the first and fifteenth of every month to make offerings, drew the bow and loosed three arrows, then poured a cup of wine.

Yue Fei first took the field at twenty. In the last years of the Northern Song, the Jin armies drove south, Bianliang was besieged, Emperor Qinzong was carried off captive, and the Prince of Kang, Zhao Gou, proclaimed himself emperor in the south — Emperor Gaozong of Song. Yue Fei served first under Zhang Suo, then under Zong Ze, fighting beside the Yellow River under Zong Ze. After Zong Ze's death he followed Du Chong; Du Chong surrendered to the Jin, but Yue Fei refused to surrender and led his men back to Jiankang. In 1130 he defeated the Jin commander Wanyan Zongbi outside Jiankang and recovered the city, and from then on he stood firm in the army.

Yue Fei had his own way of governing an army. He led by example, rewards and punishments were clear, and discipline was iron: the Yue Family Army was famed for the saying, "Freeze to death, and do not tear down a house; starve to death, and do not plunder." The Jin side conceded it too, and a saying spread among them: "It is easy to shake a mountain; it is hard to shake the Yue Family Army." From 1128 to 1140, over more than a decade, he fought hundreds of battles great and small, recovered the six prefectures of Xiang and Han, and pacified Yang Yao; the Yue Family Army grew from a little over ten thousand men to around a hundred thousand, and became the most formidable army of the Southern Song. Gaozong wrote the four characters "Jingzhong Yue Fei" — "Utmost Loyalty: Yue Fei" — in his own hand, and had them embroidered on a war banner and bestowed on him.

II. Zhuxianzhen, One Step from Bianliang

In 1140, Wanyan Wuzhu, fourth son of the Jin founder, tore up the peace agreement and attacked the Song in force. Yue Fei swept north, recovered Zhengzhou and the Luoyang region, and then crushed the Jin army at Yancheng and Yingchang. At Yancheng the Jin's "crutch cavalry" had its formation broken by the Yue army's infantry, and Wuzhu's elite took a heavy beating. Yue Fei advanced to Zhuxianzhen, a single step from Bianliang; recovering Kaifeng and bringing back the two captive emperors no longer looked like empty talk.

But Gaozong did not want to fight on. The prevailing view at the Southern Song court was for peace: fighting on cost money and carried risk, and if they won — if the two emperors came back — where would the present emperor's throne sit? No one said this layer of calculation aloud, but everyone understood it. What Yue Fei received at the front was the court's gold plaques, one after another, ordering him to bring the army home. By the commonly repeated account there were twelve in all. The exact number of times the edicts were delivered differs among the sources, and to write it down as fixed would be imprudent; but one thing is certain: the gold plaques came one after another, each more urgent than the last.

When Yue Fei received the gold plaques, he said one sentence: "The work of ten years, undone in a single morning." He withdrew all the same. The Yue Family Army returned south under orders, and the recovered land was lost again. Yue Fei came back to Lin'an and asked Gaozong to accept his resignation; Gaozong did not agree. In the fourth month of the following year the court appointed him Vice Commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs, of the first rank — an apparent promotion; in fact his military power had already been handed over. The Vice Commissioner managed the empire's military administration, but the soldiers of the Yue Family Army were no longer in his hand.

III. Vice Commissioner, Then Idleness at Mount Lu

In the seventh month of 1141, the Right Remonstrator Wanqi Xie submitted a memorial impeaching Yue Fei, saying that with his high rank and rich emolument he had grown complacent and self-satisfied and had defied imperial orders, and requesting that he be dismissed from the vice commissionership. Then the Chief Censor He Zhu and the Palace Censor Luo Rujie impeached him in turn. In the eighth month, Yue Fei was stripped of his office and went to live in idleness at Mount Lu, keeping vigil at his mother's tomb.

In the ninth month, events took a sharp turn. Wang Jun, deputy commander of the front army of the Yue Family Army at Ezhou, denounced Yue Fei to Wang Gui, saying that Yue Fei had written from Mount Lu to Zhang Xian and Yue Yun at Ezhou, intending Zhang Xian to raise troops in revolt and force the court to restore his command. In the tenth month, Yue Fei and Yue Yun, father and son, were arrested and thrown into the prison of the Court of Judicial Review. The chief judge, He Zhu, tried the case for two months and found no evidence of any crime; even He Zhu, who had himself impeached Yue Fei, turned around and said Yue Fei was suffering an injustice. Zhao Shizhuo, Prince of Qi'an, pledged his entire household of a hundred souls as guarantee that Yue Fei had no disloyal intent. Qin Hui replaced the chief judge with Wanqi Xie and went on trying — and still no evidence could be tried out of it.

Han Shizhong could not stand by and watch; he confronted Qin Hui to his face: what exactly is the crime of Yue Fei and his son? Qin Hui answered: "The letter from Fei's son Yun to Zhang Xian — although it is not clear, the matter itself: perhaps there is." That is, although the letter Yue Yun wrote to Zhang Xian could not be made out to show an intent to rebel, the thing itself probably existed. Mo xu you — three words, meaning "perhaps there is." On the strength of a "perhaps there is," a case was settled.

IV. "Perhaps There Is"

Qin Hui sent a note in his own hand to the prison authorities, and Yue Fei was put to death. On the twenty-ninth day of the twelfth month of the eleventh year of Shaoxing — January 27, 1142, New Year's Eve — Yue Fei was granted death at the Fengbo Pavilion of the Court of Judicial Review prison in Hangzhou, thirty-nine years old by the traditional count. His eldest son Yue Yun and his general Zhang Xian were beheaded the same day. Afterwards the Court of Judicial Review made up a written judgment and fixed several charges on Yue Fei: "he sat by and watched the outcome, lingering and refusing to advance"; "he spoke disrespectfully of the imperial carriage"; "he caused Zhang Xian to harbour the intent of rebellion." Deng Guangming wrote in his Biography of Yue Fei that this judgment "was composed entirely of slanderous and defamatory words and of trumped-up, fabricated events."

How exactly Yue Fei died, the histories do not entirely agree. The prevailing account is that he was granted poisoned wine. Others say his ribs were broken, or that he was granted death by strangling. His corpse was carried away at the risk of his life by the jailer Kui Shun and buried beside the Jiuqu Congci shrine outside Lin'an. Which manner of death comes closest to the truth is hard to verify today. But on one point there is no dispute: a general who had won his battles at the front did not die at the hands of the Jin; he died in the prison of his own side.

And what was the reaction on the Jin side when Yue Fei died? When the Jin commanders heard the news, they drank in celebration. Hong Hao, a Song envoy detained in Jin, sent a secret letter back to court: "The only man the Jin fear and defer to is Fei; they even call him 'father.' When their chiefs heard of his death, they poured wine and congratulated one another." The Jin envoy Liu Tao later asked a Southern Song official: "For what crime did Yue Fei die?" The official answered: "He intended rebellion and was denounced by his own general, and was executed for that." Liu Tao mocked him: "South of the river, among loyal ministers who knew how to use troops, there was only Yue Fei." Even the enemy thought it a waste.

V. After the Fengbo Pavilion

Twenty years after Yue Fei's death, in 1162, Emperor Xiaozong of Song came to the throne and issued an edict rehabilitating Yue Fei, restoring his offices and posthumous honours, and reburying him with ceremony at Qixia Ridge by West Lake. In 1178 he was given the posthumous name Wumu, "Martial and Reverent." In 1204 he was posthumously enfeoffed as Prince of E. Before the Yue Fei Temple in Hangzhou, the iron statues of Qin Hui and his wife have knelt for eight hundred years, and passers-by spit on them as they go.

The edict of rehabilitation was written plainly: "Fei rose from the ranks; in no more than a few years he attained the station of general and minister, yet he served his sovereign with loyalty, governed his troops with discipline, won merit again and again, and never boasted of it." The edict was an admission in so many words: the case of Yue Fei had been, from beginning to end, a miscarriage of justice. The Southern Song historian Li Xinchuan wrote in his Records of Court and Country Since the Jianyan Era that he had seen the court's documents of the disposition, and that the charges in the prison record were "all words forged and framed for the occasion" — from which Yue Fei's innocence can be seen.

But rehabilitation is rehabilitation; the dead do not come back to life. When Yue Fei died, the Yue Family Army of a hundred thousand men was still garrisoned at Ezhou, and the court spent twenty years breaking that army up and absorbing it. The Southern Song never again had a northern expeditionary army that dared to fight and could fight. In 1162, the very year of the rehabilitation, Emperor Xiaozong prepared a northern expedition — and in the end it never came off. Later, when the Jin wrote an edict of inducement to Wu Xi, they used Yue Fei as their argument: "Fei's fame and deeds of war were known north and south; once he fell under suspicion, he suffered execution together with his kin. How can you not be afraid?" — meaning: look at Yue Fei's end, and see whether you dare disobey.

Epilogue: Who Killed Yue Fei?

Who killed Yue Fei? To say Qin Hui killed him is not wrong: Qin Hui ran the trial, changed the judge, and passed the note — he managed it all with his own hand. To say Gaozong killed him is also not wrong: without Gaozong's nod, Qin Hui would not have dared touch a general who held a hundred thousand troops. The Jin commander Wanyan Wuzhu wrote to Qin Hui: "You beg for peace morning and night, yet Yue Fei is planning for Hebei. Fei must be killed before peace can be made." Killing Yue Fei was one of the conditions of the peace talks; it was also the sincerity Gaozong showed the Jin — and, incidentally, it could bring back his birth mother, the Empress Dowager Wei, held captive by the Jin.

So Yue Fei's death was not Qin Hui's work alone. It was the product of Gaozong's policy of appeasement combined with Qin Hui's execution of it — the two men together. To lay the whole account on Qin Hui's head, and kneel an iron statue before a temple, makes the affair look simple: a treacherous minister destroyed a loyal one, and the emperor was deceived. But real history is not so simple. Gaozong was not deceived; he did a sum. Fight on, bring back the two emperors, and his own throne is unsteady. Kill Yue Fei, and in exchange come the peace agreement, his mother's return, and a steady throne. He worked that sum out very clearly.

For eight hundred years the three words mo xu you — "perhaps there is" — have been a byword for wrongful conviction. What is terrible about them is not that they are a lie, but that they are the truth: Qin Hui did not say Yue Fei had rebelled; he said "perhaps he has." "Perhaps" was enough — enough to arrest a man, enough to convict him, enough to take his head. When a dynasty wants to kill a meritorious servant, it needs no evidence at all; all it needs is a form of words. Eight hundred years after Yue Fei's death, that form of words is still in use — only the wording has changed.
