Eyam: The Village That Walled Itself In and Waited for the Plague to Pass

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

At the end of August 1665, a parcel of cloth was sent from London to the small village of Eyam, in the hills of Derbyshire, England. The addressee was the tailor Alexander Hadfield.

London was in the grip of the Great Plague that year; in seven months it killed about 100,000 people. It was also the last great outbreak of plague in English history. The rich fled to the country, the poor hid in other counties, and the plague traveled outward with the flow of people and goods, stop by stop. The parcel Eyam received was one link in that chain. The cloth was somewhat damp, and Hadfield's assistant, George Vicars, opened it out to dry. Within a week, Vicars was dead. In Eyam's parish register, his burial is recorded on September 7, 1665 — the first name on that register's list of plague dead, number one.

No one knew the parcel hid infected fleas, and no one understood bacteria. The villagers only knew that the London sickness killed most of those it touched. Eyam was a lead-mining village; its name is written "Aium" in the Domesday Book of 1086, and lead had been dug there since Roman times. A few hundred people were scattered through the valley, living by mining, farming, and weaving, and in ordinary times they intermarried with neighboring villages, went to market, and traded — constant coming and going. The plague had entered the village with a parcel of cloth; next it would travel outward along those daily comings and goings, given only time. Autumn passed, winter came, and in the village 42 people died, on and off. The dead of the first months were still buried by the old custom, with funerals, in the churchyard. The cold slowed the sickness, and people thought the worst had passed. In the spring of 1666 the deaths climbed again: by the end of April, the register's plague dead already numbered 73.

The two men who led the decision were these. The rector, William Mompesson, had been in the post only a little over a year and was about twenty-seven. The other was Thomas Stanley, the former rector, a Puritan who had been removed from his post for refusing the rites of the established church. An Anglican rector and a Puritan were mortal enemies in that age, but in Eyam they stood together and went from house to house persuading.

In June 1666 the two men laid the plan before the villagers. Its core was a single article: the whole village would be sealed dead — no one was allowed out, and no one from outside was allowed in. The plague could rot inside that circle; it must not be carried out to harm the neighboring villages.

Persuasion was not easy. To stay was to stake the lives of one's whole family; to run was, at least for the moment, to live. Mompesson's way was to stake himself first: he promised publicly that he would not leave, that he would stay with the villagers to the end, even if he died there. Stanley did the same. With the two pastors — ordinarily at odds — leading by staying, the words could carry. According to the records that survive, in the end the whole parish accepted the plan.

The supporting rules were concrete, one by one. The village boundary was the line: markets, family visits, deliveries — all cut off. Those inside the circle did not go out; those outside did not come in; handovers took place only at designated stones and at a well, with a stretch of empty ground between that no one was allowed to cross. Worship was no longer held in the church; it moved to a natural hollow outside the village, Cucklett, where the whole village stood spaced apart to hear the sermon. The dead were no longer given funerals, nor buried in the churchyard; each family buried its own quickly, in the fields and gardens near their houses — in the faith of the time, not to be buried in consecrated ground bore on whether the soul could reach heaven, and this rule was the hardest to accept. Nor was sealing the village just a matter of closing a gate: the village's stored grain could not last more than a few months. Mompesson wrote for help to the Earl of Devonshire, at Chatsworth House; the Earl agreed to supply grain and medicine. The method of handover was this: outsiders set the goods beside a stone at the boundary and withdrew; then the villagers came to fetch them; and the coins the villagers paid were first soaked in stone holes filled with vinegar, to disinfect them. Along the boundary stood a row of such "plague stones"; the most famous is in a field on the way to Stoney Middleton, with six holes cut into it, and it is there today. The handover point on the northern boundary was a well, afterward called Mompesson's Well; vinegar water was kept ready beside it too, and coins had to pass through it before anyone dared take them.

Mompesson sent his two children to relatives in Yorkshire. His wife Catherine had at first urged her husband to take the whole family away; after the children were sent off, she changed her mind herself: she would stay, nurse the sick, and carry medicine and food from door to door.

The price of the sealing soon showed its shape. In June 21 died; in July 56; in August 78; on the worst days, five or six people had to be buried in a day. The grave-digging fell to a villager, Marshall Howe — he was not a formal gravedigger, only a man in the village still on his feet. He carried corpses every day, and in the end he lived.

The Hancock family's calamity was concentrated at the beginning of August. From August 3 to 10, in eight days, Elizabeth Hancock's husband and six children died one after another. Alone, she dragged the seven bodies up the slope behind the house, dug the pits, and buried them. She herself was not infected. That row of lonely graves, ringed in a small circle in the field, is what is now called the Riley Graves, named for her family's farm; they are now in the care of the National Trust. Across the street, the Thorpe family — nine people — died out entirely. And there was the Talbot family: the blacksmith Richard and his household died one after another, until by mid-August only a three-month-old baby was left, and in the end it could not be kept alive either. Whether one caught the sickness or not seemed a lottery: those who handled the dead daily were fine; those who never left home fell. No one could say why.

One evening in late August, Catherine was walking home with her husband when she said, on the way, that there was a sweetness in the air. By what people said at the time, for a patient to smell sweetness was a sign the organs had begun to rot. The next day she was dead, at twenty-seven. In the parish register, August 25 is her burial, number 208. Her grave remained in the churchyard, a table-style stone tomb, and every Plague Sunday someone lays a wreath on it. In letters to family and friends, Mompesson wrote that he probably would not live long either. But he and Stanley stayed to the very end.

In autumn the numbers fell: 24 in September, 18 in October. On November 1, 1666, the farm laborer Abraham Morten died of the sickness, not yet thirty — the eighteenth member of the Morten family to die in this plague, and the last. The register's plague list stopped at number 260. Between the first and the last lay fourteen months. After the plague withdrew, Mompesson had the villagers burn clothing, furniture, bedding — anything that might harbor filth — and fumigate the houses: in an age that did not know what bacteria were, it was the last disinfection they could think of.

How many people the village held at the time has no certain figure; estimates run from 350 to 800. So the proportion who died is given by some as one third, by others as two thirds. The church's records can produce 273 named dead, which does not quite square with the register's count of 260 — in a ledger three hundred years old, numbers that fail to agree are the norm, and in citing them one can only state the basis clearly. One other thing is certain: the plague never crossed that circle. Of the villages and towns around, not one had an outbreak because of Eyam. In 1669 Mompesson left Eyam to take up the living of Eakring in Nottinghamshire. The village did not die with that generation: a little over a century later Eyam's population was back above nine hundred, and the lead mines flourished again. Only the list of those fourteen months in the church register will never change.

That later ages can tell these fourteen months in such detail rests mainly on two things: that parish register, and the batch of letters Mompesson wrote during the epidemic. Without those two, Eyam would be only the name of an ordinary lead-mining village on the map.

Today's Eyam calls itself "the Plague Village." In 1994 a small museum opened in the village devoted to this history, and every year tens of thousands of visitors make the trip to see it. The row of cottages where Vicars once lived carries plaques stating who in each house died. The boundary stones still stand in the fields, and tourists occasionally leave coins in the holes. Every year on the last Sunday of August the village holds "Plague Sunday," kept since the bicentenary commemoration of 1866 down to today; the service is held at the very place where the whole village once stood spaced apart for open-air worship, the Cucklett hollow. Of the people who made the decision, most did not live to see the day the circle was lifted. Their names entered no history book; they remain only in the numbers of the parish register: number one to number two hundred and sixty.
