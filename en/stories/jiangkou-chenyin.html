The Sunken Silver of Jiangkou: Zhang Xianzhong's Treasure Lay in the River for Three Hundred Years — Then 70,000 Pieces of Gold and Silver Came Out of the Water, and the Folk Song Came True

Strange Cases · Nov 18, 2026 · ~12 min read

In Pengshan, Sichuan, there is a folk rhyme that has circulated for three hundred years: "Stone dragon facing stone tiger, gold and silver in the tens of thousands of fives; whoever can crack the secret can buy up the whole prefecture of Chengdu."

It means: where stone dragon and stone tiger face each other by the Min River, the riverbed holds gold and silver beyond counting, and whoever finds it could buy the entire prefecture of Chengdu.

This rhyme was always taken for a country legend. Until 2017, when archaeologists brought the first batch of objects up out of the river, and the legend began, bit by bit, to turn real. This March, the Jiangkou Sunken Silver Museum formally opened to the public, and its first exhibition alone displayed more than 7,000 recovered artifacts. By the end of August, visitors had already passed 320,000.

Three hundred years on, what lay at the bottom of the river has finally seen daylight. This piece tells how that treasure went down, and how it was brought back up.

I. 1646: The Fleet That Sank at Jiangkou

First, turn the clock back to 1646. By that year the Ming dynasty was already tottering, and Zhang Xianzhong's Great Western army held Chengdu.

Zhang Xianzhong is a deeply disputed figure. Some call him a leader of peasant revolt; others, a demon who slaughtered without end. The histories' accounts of what he did in Sichuan differ considerably, and this piece will not go into that; it deals only with this one affair at Jiangkou.

In 1646 Zhang Xianzhong left Chengdu with his troops and a great mass of gold, silver, and treasure, intending to sail south down the Min River. The gold and silver had been plundered over his years of campaigning — some copied out of princely palaces, some carried out of government treasuries — in quantities that stagger the imagination.

As the fleet passed the Jiangkou reach near Pengshan, it ran into an ambush by the Southern Ming general Yang Zhan. Yang Zhan had laid his men along the riverbank and struck suddenly as the fleet came through. Zhang Xianzhong's fleet was thrown into chaos on the spot; a great number of boats capsized, and the gold, silver, and treasure filling them went down into the Min River, men and cargo together.

Zhang Xianzhong himself escaped back to Chengdu. The treasure on the riverbed had sunk, just like that.

The next year, 1647, Zhang Xianzhong was shot dead by Qing troops at Fenghuang Mountain in Xichong, and the Great Western regime collapsed with him. The treasure's owner was dead, no record of the wrecks' position had been left, and from then on no one claimed the gold and silver on the riverbed.

From that day, the treasure lay at the bottom of the river for more than three hundred years, and no one saw it again.

II. For Three Hundred Years, the River Kept Spitting Out Gold

After the treasure went down, the Jiangkou area never had a quiet year again.

From the 1920s onward, cultural relics kept coming out of the Min River channel. Common folk fishing and panning sand along the banks would now and then haul up silver ingots and gold objects. By the 1990s, more and more Ming silver ingots and gold and silver wares were being unearthed around Pengshan, and the saying that "there is treasure at Jiangkou" was known to practically everyone in the area.

In 2005, another batch of Ming silver ingots came out of the Min River channel at Jiangkou. When the news spread, even more people came to the riverbanks to "hunt treasure." There were proper archaeologists, and there were looters who slipped into the river in the middle of the night.

Through all these years, the "treasure hunting" along the river never stopped. Some swept the gravel bars with metal detectors; some went into the water at night to grope by hand. The more objects came out, the more disorderly things became. How many relics were dug away by thieves can no longer be counted. After the great looting case of 2014, the authorities resolved on a rescue excavation — and the direct cause was exactly this: wait any longer, and there would be nothing left on the riverbed.

In 2011, a single torn page of a gold register of investiture came out of the river channel. A gold register was the document an emperor used to enfeoff an imperial consort, made of gold; this one was authenticated as a grade-one national treasure. Once it appeared, "Zhang Xianzhong's sunken silver" was no longer mere legend — a gold register that could go down with a ship showed that the story of the sinking was true.

But what truly pushed the matter into the storm's eye was a huge looting case in 2014.

That year, a gang of tomb robbers slipped into the Min River and looted relics on a large scale. The police later spent two years on the case, arrested 70 people, and recovered more than a thousand artifacts. The sums involved, according to reports, reached 300 million yuan.

The affair blew up, and the cultural relics authorities could sit still no longer. Without a rescue excavation, what lay on the riverbed would sooner or later be stolen clean. In January 2017, the first season of archaeological excavation at the Jiangkou sunken silver site formally began.

III. Cofferdams and Pumps: Archaeologists "Dig" a Treasure at the Bottom of a River

Underwater archaeology is an entirely different business from digging in earth on land. The river never stops flowing; the relics lie buried in silt, invisible and untouchable.

The method the archaeologists used was the cofferdam: first build an embankment in the river to enclose a stretch of the channel, then pump the water inside dry so the riverbed is exposed, and then dig down layer by layer, just as on land.

From January to April 2017 the first season closed with more than 30,000 artifacts recovered. Once that number came out, the "Jiangkou sunken silver" affair stood confirmed — the treasure the folk rhyme spoke of was real.

The second season ran from January 2018, and in three months brought out more than 12,000 pieces. The third season ran from January to April 2020 and produced more than 10,000 pieces, among them the object that later became the most famous of all: the gold seal "Treasure of the Heir of the Prince of Shu."

Across six seasons of excavation, from April 2016 to April 2023, the cumulative recovery came to more than 70,000 artifacts. What does that number mean? Laid out piece by piece, they could fill several exhibition halls.

Among the relics are gold and silver coins of the "Reward of the King of the West," a gold seal with a tiger knob inscribed "Grand Marshal of Yongchang," gold registers for the investiture of consorts, and case after case of silver ingots. The ingots most commonly follow the standards of ten taels, twenty taels, and fifty taels (a tael, the standard unit for silver, is about 37.3 grams). The largest of them all, a "Principal Silver of the Waterworks Office, One Hundred Taels," weighs about 3,700 grams, and is the largest Ming-dynasty silver ingot known to survive.

The site was afterwards selected among the Top Ten Archaeological Discoveries of 2017, and again among the "Hundred Great Archaeological Discoveries of the Century." A three-hundred-year legend was dug, spadeful by spadeful, into settled fact by the archaeologists.

These silver ingots carry a meaning that gold itself cannot give. Every ingot is chiselled with an inscription stating its place of origin, its year, and the names of the officials and silversmiths who handled it. That largest ingot, the "Principal Silver of the Waterworks Office, One Hundred Taels," bears on its face the words: "Principal silver of the Waterworks Office, duly collected for the twenty-seventh year of Wanli, one hundred taels exactly," followed by the names of Ying Nan, magistrate of Wugang Prefecture, of the clerk who handled it, and of the silversmith.

Which is to say: these ingots are not merely money. They are page after page of the Ming dynasty's fiscal ledger. From which prefecture and county the silver was collected, through whose hands it passed, in which year it was cast into an ingot — all of it is written on them. What Zhang Xianzhong seized in his day was not just gold and silver; it was the entire tax-silver reserve of the Ming. Every ingot hauled up from the riverbed testifies on behalf of an accounts clerk of three hundred years ago.

IV. A Gold Seal Broken into Five Pieces

Of the more than 70,000 artifacts, the most poignant is the gold seal, "Treasure of the Heir of the Prince of Shu."

This gold seal came out during the third season of excavation in 2020. It is the gold treasure-seal of a Ming imperial prince's heir — the first physical specimen of such a seal ever found in the country. By weight alone it ranks among the foremost of the recovered relics.

But when it came out of the water, it was broken. The seal was found already split into five pieces, scattered in different places. The archaeologists spent a long time fitting the five fragments back together and restoring it into a complete gold seal.

The fate of this seal happens to be the epitome of the whole Jiangkou affair: the things are real, and when they went down they broke, scattered, and were buried — only three hundred years later to be found again, piece by piece.

There is also the gold seal with the tiger knob, "Grand Marshal of Yongchang," weighing 3.195 kilograms. In 1646 it too went down into the riverbed with the wrecks. The "Yongchang" on the seal is Zhang Xianzhong's era name. A single seal, a little over half a jin in weight, bearing the era name of a short-lived dynasty.

Displayed in a museum, these things are beautiful to look at. But their true worth is not the gold itself; it is what they can prove: that the battle at Jiangkou in 1646 really was fought, that the fleet really sank, and that the legend of three hundred years ago was not invented.

V. What Kind of Man Was Zhang Xianzhong?

At this point we have to come back to Zhang Xianzhong himself. For whenever Jiangkou's sunken silver is mentioned, the argument always starts: was Zhang Xianzhong a hero, or a butcher?

First, what can be established. Zhang Xianzhong was a leader of the peasant armies of the late Ming, born in 1606; in 1644 he founded the Great Western regime at Chengdu, with the era name Dashun. In 1646 he left Chengdu and moved south; in 1647 he was shot dead by Qing troops at Fenghuang Mountain in Xichong. These points the official histories and local gazetteers agree on.

The dispute concerns the years when he ruled Sichuan. One account holds that he slaughtered on a vast scale in Sichuan, massacring city after city; another holds that the numbers of the massacres were exaggerated when the Qing compiled the histories, to lend majesty to the new dynasty. Both accounts have documentary support, both have their doubters, and to this day there is no settled conclusion.

This piece takes no side; it makes only one point: however Zhang Xianzhong the man is judged, the matter of the Jiangkou sunken silver itself has already been answered by archaeology. The gold and silver are real, the shipwreck is real, and the folk rhyme's talk of "gold and silver in the tens of thousands of fives" — exaggerated as it is — rests on a true foundation.

A man's reputation can be argued over for three hundred years. The truth or falsehood of an artifact is known the moment it is dug up. That is the fairest thing about archaeology.

Epilogue: The Legend Has Come to Ground

At the end of 2025, the Jiangkou Sunken Silver Museum was completed. On March 8 this year it formally opened to the public, its first exhibition displaying more than 7,000 artifacts. By the end of August, visitors had passed 320,000, and revenue had exceeded 13 million yuan.

The museum has three permanent galleries, with names as plain as they come: "The Waters Fall, the Silver Emerges," "Unraveling the Sunken Silver," and "Stories of Silver." The first gallery tells how the objects were brought up; the second, how archaeology confirmed the legend step by step; the third, the Ming-dynasty stories behind the gold and silver. Walk through them once, and you have walked through this whole story.

It is the country's only museum devoted to gold and silver wares. It stands in Jiangkou Town, where the Fu River and the Nan River meet, not far from where the ships went down.

Three hundred years ago, a folk rhyme circulated along the Min River: "Stone dragon facing stone tiger, gold and silver in the tens of thousands of fives; whoever can crack the secret can buy up the whole prefecture of Chengdu."

Three hundred years later, the answer was revealed. The ones who cracked it were not treasure hunters, but a team of archaeologists who built cofferdams in the river, pumped the water dry, and dug down layer by layer.

A legend fears nothing so much as being taken seriously. Take it seriously, and it either shatters — or comes true. The sunken silver of Jiangkou came true.
