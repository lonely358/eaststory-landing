The Pastry War: A Pastry Chef Demanded 60,000 Pesos, and France Really Sent a Fleet to Fight Mexico

World Curiosities · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In 1838, France and Mexico went to war, and the cause of it was a pastry shop. Remontel, a French pastry chef, kept a shop outside Mexico City; the shop was looted by Mexican officers, and he petitioned the King of France for 60,000 pesos in compensation. His shop itself was valued at less than 1,000 pesos. The King of France decided this was a fine pretext, and sent a fleet to blockade Mexico and bombard Veracruz. The war has been known ever since as the Pastry War.

I. A Pastry Shop, Looted

Remontel was a Frenchman who kept a pastry shop in Tacubaya, a suburb of Mexico City. In 1832, a party of Mexican officers came into his shop and looted it. The Mexican version of the story is somewhat different: they said the officers had merely eaten their fill and left without paying — it was not a looting. Whether it was looting or dining without paying, the sources preserve both versions side by side, and no verdict will be made here.

Remontel would not swallow the loss. He petitioned Louis-Philippe, King of France, claiming 60,000 pesos. The figure was outrageous: his shop was valued at less than 1,000 pesos, and the claim was more than sixty times the value of the shop. But he did not care, and the French government did not care either. France had a whole pile of claims by its nationals in Mexico at the time: in 1828, French shops in the Parian market were looted; in 1837, a French citizen was executed as a pirate. Remontel's pastry shop was the smallest case of them all — it just happened to have the most interesting name.

The French in Mexico did not have an easy time of it. After independence, Mexico's politics were in turmoil; foreign merchants' shops were often wrecked in riots, and claims for compensation were taken up by no one. Remontel was not the first Frenchman to suffer such a loss; his case was simply the smallest, and the one with the best story to it, and the French government chose him.

Mexico had only recently become independent; its factions were many, civil strife was constant, and ordinary people whose property was destroyed had nowhere to seek redress — and foreign residents were in the same position. France had long wanted to establish itself in Mexico: France was Mexico's third-largest trading partner, yet French goods had to pay higher duties than British or American ones, because France had never managed to sign an equal trade treaty with Mexico. A claim over a pastry shop became a ready-made pretext.

Of Remontel himself, the record preserves very little. All that is known is that his surname was Remontel, that he was a French pastry chef, and that his shop stood in Tacubaya; even his full name has not come down to us. A man who did not leave even his full name behind gave a war its name. History is sometimes like this: the man who steps forward is not necessarily the protagonist, and the protagonist does not necessarily leave a name.

II. A Bill for 600,000 Pesos

In 1838, Molé, the French prime minister, presented Mexico with the bill: compensation of 600,000 pesos, equivalent to three million francs. The money included Remontel's 60,000 pesos, and the losses of other French nationals besides.

Bustamante, the President of Mexico, would not pay. The King of France then gave his orders: Rear Admiral Baudin was to lead a fleet, blockade every port on the Gulf of Mexico from Yucatán to the Rio Grande, bombard the fortress of San Juan de Ulúa at the port of Veracruz, and take that most important port city on the Mexican Gulf.

On November 27, the French guns opened fire. The fortress was soon taken, and in December the French occupied Veracruz. Mexico declared war on France. The French had 3,000 men, the Mexicans more than 3,200; when the fighting was done, French casualties numbered 121, and Mexican casualties 224.

Once trade was cut off, the Mexicans took to smuggling, bringing imported goods in secretly through Corpus Christi, in Texas. Texas patrols intercepted the smuggling boats in the bay, and one band of smugglers abandoned about a hundred barrels of flour and fled; the place has been called Flour Bluff ever since. One smuggling story left another name on the map. The United States even sent a schooner to help France keep up the blockade. A war fought over a pastry shop was beginning to look more and more like the real thing.

III. Santa Anna's Leg

With the war at this point, Mexico needed a man who could fight. That man was Antonio López de Santa Anna.

Santa Anna had already retired and was living on his estate near Jalapa. When the French came, he came out of retirement of his own accord, and the government ordered him to resist the French "by every means." He led Mexican troops into action against the French at Veracruz, and in a small clash with the French rear guard his leg was struck by French grapeshot, and had to be amputated.

That lost leg later became Santa Anna's political capital. The leg was buried with full military honors, and Santa Anna himself made great use of his wound in publicity, returning to the center of power. A general who has lost a leg for his country is hard for ordinary people not to hold in esteem. Santa Anna owed his comeback more to that leg than to any fighting he won. Afterward, his life rose and fell again and again; he served as President of Mexico many times over and became one of the most famous political figures in Mexican history. By one count, he held the presidency eleven times in all. Each term was short, but the number itself speaks for the weight he carried in Mexican politics.

As for the grandeur of the leg's burial, later accounts contain exaggeration. The version supported by the record is simply that it was buried with military honors — and that, without embellishment, is all that will be said here.

IV. The Treaty, and the Indemnity That Was Never Paid

On March 9, 1839, under British mediation, France and Mexico signed a peace treaty. Mexico agreed to pay the 600,000 pesos in installments, and the French forces withdrew. The war had lasted more than three months and killed more than three hundred men — all for one pastry shop and one bill.

But the indemnity, in the end, was never paid. And the unpaid debt did not go to waste: in 1861, France took it as one of its pretexts and launched the second intervention in Mexico. This time France did not merely send a fleet to blockade the ports; it sent an army outright, and installed the Austrian Archduke Maximilian as Emperor of Mexico. In 1867 the Mexicans won that war, and Maximilian was executed. In 1870 the Second Empire collapsed; only in 1880 did France and Mexico restore diplomatic relations, both sides at last abandoning the claims connected with the two wars.

From one pastry shop, to two military interventions, to more than forty years of broken relations — a claim of 60,000 pesos drew half a century of diplomatic history behind it. Remontel probably never dreamed that his shop, worth less than 1,000 pesos, would leave so large a name in history.

V. A Small Claim, a Large Pretext

The Pastry War is not the only war in history fought over a small thing. The Pig War was fought over a pig, the Emu War over a flock of birds, the War of the Bucket over a wooden bucket. These wars have one thing in common: the smaller the cause, the clearer it becomes that the real reason does not lie in the cause.

What France wanted was never the 60,000 pesos, nor the 600,000 either. It wanted trade privileges in Mexico, and a presence in Latin America. Mexico's refusal to pay merely gave France a reason to open fire. Remontel's shop was that reason.

This kind of thing recurs throughout history: when a great power wants to move, it can always find a pretext. The more absurd the pretext, the clearer it is that the move was long prepared. The looting of the pastry shop was real, the claim of 60,000 pesos was real, and the French fleet's bombardment was real — only one thing was false, and that was that the French truly cared about a pastry shop.

Epilogue: The Price of a Pastry

When the Pastry War was over, the French had learned a lesson, and the Mexicans had learned one too. The French discovered that what is taken by gunboat cannot be held; the Mexicans discovered that a debt left unpaid comes back sooner or later, with interest added.

Looking back on this war today, what most repays thought is still that contrast of numbers: a shop worth less than 1,000 pesos, a claim of 60,000 pesos. A gap of sixty times is exactly the whole logic of a war — the losses of a little man can be magnified without limit, as long as the great men need a reason to start one.

One pastry, in the end, was priced at 600,000 pesos, plus more than three hundred lives. It is probably the most expensive pastry in history. What became of Remontel's shop afterward, the record does not say. He probably received his compensation — or perhaps he did not. But his name is bound up with a war now, and that endures longer than any pastry. The next time you hear of some country going to war over a trifle, it is worth asking first: is what it truly wants really the thing written on the bill?
