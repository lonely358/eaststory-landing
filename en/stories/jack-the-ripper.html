Jack the Ripper: Five Women Murdered in Whitechapel in Ten Weeks — and After More Than a Century, Still No One Knows Who Did It

World Curiosities · Nov 18, 2026 · ~10 min read

In the autumn of 1888, in Whitechapel, in London's East End, five women died within ten weeks.

Five people, killed in much the same way: their throats cut open, their abdomens cut open. The places where the bodies were found were all within a few streets of one another. The newspapers chased the story every day, and all of London was asking: who is the killer?

More than a hundred and thirty years have passed, and there is still no answer. The case became the most famous unsolved case in the world, and its name is "Jack the Ripper."

I. What Kind of Place Was Whitechapel?

To tell this case, you first have to talk about Whitechapel.

Whitechapel was a poor district in London's East End. By 1888 some 80,000 people lived there — crowded, filthy, and badly policed. More than half of the children born in the East End did not live to the age of five. Robbery, drunkenness, and violence were everyday life there.

By the Metropolitan Police's estimate in October of that year, Whitechapel had 62 brothels, and 1,200 women made their living by prostitution. Far more people lived in common lodging houses, where a bed for the night cost four pence; those who had no money for a bed could lean against a rope for the night for two pence.

Whitechapel had become what it was over decades. From the middle of the nineteenth century, large numbers of Irish immigrants had poured into London's East End; after 1882, waves of Jewish refugees arrived, fleeing the pogroms in Russia. More and more people, housing more and more crowded, work harder and harder to find. Poverty was the underlying color of Whitechapel.

In a place like that, a death was not ordinarily news. In fact, before Nichols was murdered, Whitechapel had already seen two killings that year: Emma Smith in April and Martha Tabram in August. Tabram's body bore 39 stab wounds, and she died just as cruelly. But these two cases were not counted among the "canonical five," because the manner of their deaths did not match the five that followed, and most experts believe they were not the work of the "Ripper."

But these five cases were different.

II. Ten Weeks, Five People

The first happened in the early hours of August 31, 1888. The body of Mary Ann Nichols was found on Buck's Row, her throat deeply cut twice, her abdomen cut open, her entrails outside her body. The last time anyone saw her alive was about an hour earlier, walking toward Whitechapel Road.

A week later, on the morning of September 8, the body of Annie Chapman was found in the backyard of 29 Hanbury Street. She died as Nichols had: her throat cut, her abdomen opened entirely, her uterus and part of her bladder and vagina taken away.

In the early hours of September 30, two women died in a single night. The body of Elizabeth Stride was found on Berner Street, her throat severed by a single cut, but with no other wounds on her body — many suspect the killer was interrupted. Less than an hour later, the body of Catherine Eddowes was found in Mitre Square: throat cut, abdomen opened, the left kidney and most of the uterus taken away, and her face mutilated as well. That night of two corpses later came to be called the "double event."

The last case came on the morning of November 9. The body of Mary Jane Kelly was found in the room she rented at 13 Miller's Court, dead in the most terrible state of the five: her face hacked beyond recognition, her throat cut down to the spine, her abdomen nearly emptied out, her uterus, kidneys, and one breast placed beneath her head, and her heart gone.

Five people, ten weeks. The police attributed these five cases to a single killer and called them the "canonical five." But to be clear about it: this grouping is a convention of the police and of later historians, not a scientific conclusion. In the Whitechapel murders file there were eleven murders in all between 1888 and 1891, and whether they were truly the work of one person has never been established to this day.

III. That Letter, and This Name

The killer's name came from a letter.

At the end of September 1888, a letter signed "Jack the Ripper" arrived at a news agency; it opened with the words "Dear Boss." The writer claimed to be the killer himself, in a taunting tone, with vivid details. Once the newspapers printed the letter, the name "Jack the Ripper" spread across London overnight.

But the authenticity of this letter was doubted from the very beginning. Historians generally believe it was very likely forged by a journalist to sell newspapers. In those days, newspapers lived by circulation, and a string of murders plus a letter from the murderer could multiply sales several times over.

There is another, more famous letter, called "From Hell." It was sent to George Lusk, the head of the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, and enclosed with it was half of a preserved human kidney, which the writer claimed had been taken from one of the victims. The authenticity of this letter is likewise unsettled. The police took it seriously for a time and had the kidney examined, but the examination could not establish very much.

And so the name was fixed. A signature that may never have existed at all became a very real symbol of terror.

IV. Why He Was Never Caught

The case happened in 1888, and the means of solving crimes then were nothing like today's.

There was no fingerprinting, no DNA, no surveillance cameras. Crime scenes were often surrounded by onlookers, who trampled the footprints into confusion. An autopsy could tell the police how a victim had died; it could not answer who the killer was.

It was not that the police did not try. The Metropolitan Police put large numbers of officers into the streets, searched house by house, questioned more than a thousand people, and offered rewards. The residents of Whitechapel organized a vigilance committee of their own, sent men out to patrol the streets at night, and stopped and questioned anyone who looked suspicious.

But Whitechapel had too transient a population. Many people had no fixed address and no registration of identity; once questioned, they could not be found again. Worse still, the newspapers reported the case every day and the whole city talked of nothing else — so the killer, as long as he could read, could learn from the papers exactly how far the police had got.

After the murders began, the circulation of London's newspapers soared, and "the Ripper" became the talk of the city. Some people wrote to the papers claiming to be the killer; some went about impersonating him; the police received more than a hundred "letters from the murderer," most of them hoaxes. With truth and lies mixed together, the investigation grew harder by another layer.

More troublesome still, the case itself may not have been one man's work. Among the five victims, Stride had no abdominal wounds, which does not match the method of the other four. Some scholars believe she may not have been killed by "the Ripper" at all, but simply happened to die in the same period and the same district. When even the question of whether the five were killed by the same person has no settled answer in scholarship, there is hardly anywhere for an investigation to begin.

One more detail is worth a sentence of its own. In the older telling, all five victims were taken for granted to be sex workers. But in recent years scholars have re-examined their lives and found that only Kelly can be confirmed to have been engaged in sex work at the time; the circumstances of the others were a good deal more complicated. They were first of all poor people — women of the lowest stratum, living in common lodging houses and scraping by on casual work. To lump them simply into a single category is neither accurate nor fair.

V. More Than a Century On, They Are Still Looking for Him

The case was never solved, but the people looking for the killer have never stopped coming.

In more than a hundred and thirty years, over a hundred theories of suspects have appeared: a member of the royal family, a surgeon, a butcher, a barber, a Polish immigrant, an American doctor... Every theory has people who believe it, and not one of them can be proven.

Some of the theories sound very plausible. Some say the killer was Prince Albert Victor of the royal family, because he had served in the navy and his movements fit; some say he was a surgeon, because the way the organs were removed was too deft, as though he had been trained in anatomy; others say he was a butcher, with knife skills practiced on livestock. But every one of these accounts has holes when examined closely: the prince has an alibi; anatomical knowledge was not rare among the butchers and surgical students of the time; and the judgment of "deft knife work" itself rests on incomplete autopsy records.

In 2019, someone announced that DNA technology had "solved" the case, naming the killer as Aaron Kosminski, a Polish immigrant. The news made plenty of headlines at the time, but the scholarly world does not accept it. The source of the DNA sample, the testing method, and the reliability of the conclusion are all disputed. To this day, that claim has still not won general acceptance.

This is what makes the Ripper case so singular: it is not a case that is "nearly solved," but a case that can never be solved. The evidence was already fragmentary in 1888, and the longer time passes, the more of it goes missing.

And yet precisely because it cannot be solved, it stays alive. Novels, films, television dramas, and documentaries tell the story over and over again. And every telling brings a new batch of readers into the ranks of the "armchair detectives."

Epilogue: A Case with No Answer

After November 9, 1888, the murders in Whitechapel stopped. Whether the killer died, moved away, or simply stopped, no one knows.

The names of the five women, at least, have come down to us: Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes, Mary Jane Kelly.

More than a hundred and thirty years later, what people remember is mostly the killer's resounding name, rather than the names of these five people. That in itself is the most uncomfortable thing about this case.

Today's Whitechapel is long past what it was in 1888. Mitre Square, Hanbury Street, and Miller's Court are all still there, and along the streets there are even guided "Ripper tours," leading visitors from one crime scene to the next. The case was never solved, but the tourist trade got going. That, in itself, gives one something to think about.

An unsolvable case is not necessarily without meaning. It reminds those who come after: if a society cannot protect even the people at its very bottom, tragedy will not happen only once. The lesson of Whitechapel lies not in how cunning the killer was, but in the circumstances of those women — living in the cheapest lodging houses, doing the most fragmentary casual work, walking the streets alone at night. It was not that they had no sense of danger; it was that they had no other choice. And every retelling of the story should first treat the victims as people, not as footnotes to a mystery.
