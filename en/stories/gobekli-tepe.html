The Vulture Stone: A Disaster Carved into a Pillar, or Have Later Generations Read Too Much into It?

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In October 1994, the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt, carrying a survey report more than thirty years old, climbed a limestone hill in southeastern Turkey.

The report had been left by an archaeological survey in 1963. The surveyors had seen large stone slabs jutting from the ground on this hill, taken them for tombstones of a medieval cemetery, registered them, and left. Schmidt had previously excavated Neolithic ritual structures at a site nearby, and he knew a thing called a T-shaped pillar. Standing on the slope, looking at the stone chips and flints scattered over the surface, he made his judgment on the spot: what lay buried under this hill was not a cemetery.

The following year, excavation began. This place, called in Turkish "the pot-bellied hill" — Göbekli Tepe — would go on, little by little, to rewrite the sequence of civilization's origins as the textbooks told it.

First, what came out of the ground. Enclosure after enclosure, circular or oval, their stone walls set with T-shaped limestone pillars, and at the center of each, standing, the two largest, about 5.5 meters high and weighing more than ten tons. The pillars are anthropomorphic: the central pair are carved with arms, a belt, and a loincloth, and the pillar shafts carry reliefs of foxes, wild boars, snakes, cranes, and scorpions. Radiocarbon dating places the earliest enclosures around 9500 BC.

That date is worth pausing on. At that time there was no pottery, no metal, no writing, and the people of the region had not truly begun to farm. The builders were hunter-gatherers. They quarried stone from the hill itself, shaped it, and raised pillars of more than ten tons; and when an enclosure was retired, they set to work and carefully buried the whole structure. Geophysical survey shows that at least twenty more enclosures still lie buried in the hill; what has been excavated so far is only a small part. In 2018, the site was inscribed on the World Heritage List.

One more thing puzzles archaeologists to this day: these enclosures did not collapse — they were buried. When a period of use ended, the builders' descendants cleared the enclosure out, dismantled some of its components, and then backfilled it whole with earth and rubble, the fill mixed with large quantities of animal bones and objects evidently held to be significant. The backfilling happened quite quickly; it does not look like natural accumulation. From the raising of its pillars to its sealing, an enclosure seems to have run through a ritual process with a beginning and an end.

Schmidt, in his lifetime, made a famous judgment: the usual narrative of civilization runs agriculture first, settlement first, and only then the surplus to build temples; Göbekli Tepe may have reversed that order — it was the periodic great feasts and building works that first drew scattered hunting bands together, forcing them to learn cooperation, grain storage, and the organization of labor, and agriculture was something "forced" into being afterward. That judgment has never been settled, but it makes one thing clear: in organizational capacity, these people were far beyond anything the word "primitive" can dismiss.

Schmidt died in 2014. Of the questions he left behind, the most striking hangs on one pillar in Enclosure D.

Pillar 43 stands set into the wall on the northwest side of Enclosure D, its surface carved so densely there is hardly a gap. Read it from the bottom up: at the very bottom is the head of a long-necked great bird, like a duck, a goose, or a swan; above it, a scorpion; above the scorpion, a vulture with its wings spread, the left wing raised upright, the right wing stretched out level; to the vulture's right, a bird like a crane or a flamingo, its body bent in the middle at an obtuse angle; further right, a snake wriggling downward — or a fish; to the scorpion's left, the head and forelegs of a dog or a wolf emerge. At the very bottom of the shaft is carved a small headless man. Above the whole company of animals run two rows of nested V-shaped patterns; at the very top stand side by side three "handbag"-shaped designs, each accompanied by a small animal: a bird with a bent neck, a horned four-legged beast, and a reptile like a frog. At the visual center of the whole scene, above the vulture's right wing, is carved a disk.

The excavation team's mainstream reading of this scene has to do with burial. The vulture is a carrion eater, and in the thought of the ancient Near East it was bound up with the treatment of corpses; human skulls that were deliberately cut and drilled have been excavated at Göbekli Tepe, which researchers call a "skull cult." A headless man together with a vulture was a recurring motif in that region at that time, pointing to a multi-stage funerary rite: defleshing, the skull, the bird. This is the reading the archaeological evidence can support — what is in the picture, and what these images usually meant in the culture of the time.

In 2017, another reading forced its way in. Its authors were Martin Sweatman and Dimitrios Tsikritsis of the University of Edinburgh's School of Engineering. Both men are engineers, not archaeologists. In a paper in Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry, they argued that the animals on the pillar are not animals but constellations. Each animal corresponds to a star group, and the disk is the sun. Feed the positions of these "constellations" into software, run precession — the slow wobble of the Earth's axis over tens of thousands of years — backward, and the whole picture points to a date: 10,950 BC, give or take 250 years.

That year was not chosen at random. Geologists had long known that about 12,900 years ago, halfway through the warming out of the ice age, the Earth suddenly plunged back into deep cold for more than a thousand years — the Younger Dryas, as it is called. In the Greenland ice cores, that layer shows an anomalous spike of platinum. From 2007 onward, one school of scholars put forward the hypothesis that fragments of a comet had struck the Earth. Sweatman held that Pillar 43 is the "date stamp" of that catastrophe: the headless man is one of the disaster's dead; the whole site was an observatory for watching meteor showers; and what the builders were watching was the Taurid meteor stream — which, on the impact account, is the very channel through which the wreckage of that giant comet still visits the Earth today. The comet's parent body is conjectured to have been a precursor of Comet Encke, some 100 kilometers across. Carried around the world by the books and programs of the writer Graham Hancock and others, the story that "a higher civilization existed more than ten thousand years ago and was wiped out by a comet" grew out of this one pillar.

The excavation team's reply appeared in the same journal the same year, under the title "More than a Vulture" — restrained in tone, unrestrained in content. The objections are four. First, the dates do not line up: the oldest radiocarbon dates obtainable for Enclosure D come from the wall plaster and run to about 9745–9314 BC, seven hundred to a thousand years later than Sweatman's date — the site's age falls at the tail of the Younger Dryas, not at its onset. Second, the positions of the pillars are unreliable: many pillars were pulled out and reused, and the buildings may have had roofs — and how does a roofed house serve as an observatory? Third, the constellation mapping is unreliable: matching the animals one by one to the constellations in use today amounts to assuming that Anatolian hunters of more than ten thousand years ago and the Egyptians and Greeks who came later were all looking at the same star map. Fourth, cherry-picking: more than sixty pillars are known at the site, each with its own emphasis of imagery; singling out one pillar to decipher, and peeling it out of the whole image system, is itself a problem. As for the headless man, the excavation team points out that the figure is carved with unmistakable male genitalia at its lower body, which in a funerary motif carries another meaning; reading him as a "victim of the disaster" is altogether too convenient. And the disk: in the eyes of the funerary reading, it more likely has to do with the head carried off by the vulture — with the soul — than with the sun. One picture, two complete readings, and neither side can persuade the other.

On the geological side, the comet-impact hypothesis itself is far from settled. The markers its supporters cite — nanodiamonds, magnetic spherules, high-temperature melt glass — have each been given non-impact explanations by its critics. That argument has run from 2007 to the present without a conclusion.

So what can now be separated falls into three layers. The layer of evidence: the pillars, the reliefs, the dates, the burial — all real; hunter-gatherers raised them around 9500 BC. The layer of hypothesis: Sweatman's constellation reading is a formally published paper with a complete chain of reasoning, but the excavation team refuted it point by point in the same journal, and the mainstream of archaeology does not accept it. The layer of popular narrative: the "lost higher civilization" of Hancock's version does not exist in the paper — Sweatman speaks throughout of the astronomical observations of hunter-gatherers, and promoting the pillar's makers into a lost civilization is a flourish the popular retelling added itself.

Pillar 43 still stands in its original place in Enclosure D, a protective shelter built over its head. The vulture, the scorpion, the headless man, and that disk are all there. Whether it is a funerary diagram, a star map, or the memorial of a catastrophe, no side can produce evidence that will shut the other up. More than eleven thousand years ago, a people who did not yet even have clay pots carved the thing they held most important into stone. What they carved, they left no manual to say.
