Joan of Arc: At Seventeen She Led an Army to Save Her Country; at Nineteen She Was Burned at the Stake in Rouen

World Curiosities · Nov 19, 2026 · ~10 min read

At thirteen, she said she had heard the voices of saints. At seventeen, she led an army and lifted the siege of Orléans. At nineteen, she was bound to a stake and burned to death. Twenty-five years later, the Church itself reversed her condemnation. More than four hundred years after that, she was formally canonized a saint. Joan's life lasted only nineteen years, yet later ages have been telling it for six hundred. What kind of girl she was has to be told from the beginning.

I. A Farm Girl from Domrémy

Joan was born on or about January 6, 1412, in Domrémy, a small village in northeastern France. Her father, Jacques d'Arc, was a farmer who also held a minor post as a tax collector in the village; the family had fifty acres of land and lived comfortably enough. She could not read or write and had had no schooling — an ordinary farm girl.

By her own account, at the age of thirteen, beneath the great tree behind the village, she encountered Saint Michael, Saint Margaret, and Saint Catherine, and heard voices bidding her lead an army to recover the French lands occupied by England. Whether this "revelation" was a genuine religious experience, a hallucination, or something else has never been settled in six hundred years. In writing about it, all one can honestly say is "by her own account" — not that she truly heard anything.

The France of Joan's lifetime was at its worst moment. The Hundred Years' War had been going on for nearly a hundred years; great stretches of northern France were occupied by England; King Charles VI was mentally deranged; and at court the Burgundian and Armagnac factions had been at each other's throats for thirty years. The Dauphin Charles held only the territory south of the Loire, and even Reims, the place of coronation, was in enemy hands. In 1429, the English had been besieging Orléans for seven months. That city was the last gate barring the English advance south; if it fell, France was finished.

II. The Nine Days of Orléans

At sixteen, Joan went to Robert de Baudricourt, the commander of the local garrison, and said she wanted to see the Dauphin and lead an army to relieve Orléans. Baudricourt mocked her at first; later, she foretold the French defeat at the Battle of the Herrings, the prediction came true, and only then did he agree to escort her to Charles. Charles received her at Chinon. It is said that, to test her, Charles deliberately mingled among the nobles, and Joan picked him out at a glance. This detail comes from later accounts; whether it is true is hard to judge, and it is best taken as no more than a story. Charles's mother-in-law Yolande raised the money to put together an expeditionary force, and Joan put on armor and set out carrying her banner.

On April 29, 1429, Joan's army reached Orléans. The French commanders had intended first to resupply the city; Joan insisted on attacking directly. She rode at the very front of every skirmish, holding that conspicuous banner. On May 7, during the assault on Les Tourelles, an arrow struck her in the shoulder; soldiers carried her out of the line, but she soon pulled the arrow out herself and returned to the battle. That day the French took the stone fort that commanded the bridge, and the next day the English raised the siege. Orléans was relieved.

Historians have always debated Joan's actual role on the battlefield. Some hold that she was chiefly a symbol, a source of morale; others, on the testimony of her subordinates, hold that she was a resourceful tactician. Either way, the French army under her won a string of victories — that is a fact. Once the siege of Orléans was lifted, the whole course of the war turned. The French pressed their advantage, and in June took Jargeau, Beaugency, and several other towns in succession. The Battle of Patay on June 18 was brilliantly fought: the French vanguard launched its attack before the English longbowmen could form their lines, killing and capturing great numbers of English soldiers, while the English commander Fastolf escaped with only a handful of men. Later ages have regarded this battle as the reversal of Agincourt: French losses were very small, and the English were badly mauled.

III. The Coronation at Reims

After Orléans, Joan insisted on marching on Reims. Reims was the traditional place for the coronation of French kings, but it lay far from Orléans, deep in enemy territory. The French army advanced north, and the towns along the way surrendered one after another without a fight. On July 16, 1429, Reims opened its gates, and early the next morning Charles was crowned Charles VII in Reims Cathedral, with Joan standing at his side.

The weight of that moment was clear at the time: a dauphin who had not been crowned was only half a king; once crowned, he was the rightful King of France in name and in fact. By setting Charles on the throne, Joan had in effect won back the legitimacy of France. On the English side stood Henry VI, nine months old, nominally King of France as well — but his coronation had not yet been held, and in sheer presence he had already lost a step.

After the coronation, Joan pressed to go on and attack Paris, but Charles preferred to negotiate with Burgundy. On September 8 the French attacked Paris; Joan was wounded in the leg, and early the next morning Charles ordered the retreat. Many historians regard that retreat as a turning point: from then on, Joan's weight at court began to decline, and she was no longer the person whose word always carried.

IV. The Moment the Gate of Compiègne Closed

On May 23, 1430, Joan was captured in a small clash near Compiègne. She was at the very rear of the force, covering the army's withdrawal into the city. The garrison of Compiègne, fearing the English would charge in behind the retreating troops, closed the gate before everyone had got back inside. Joan and the rear guard were left outside the walls and fell into the hands of the Burgundian army.

By the custom of the time, a prisoner could be ransomed: the family paid money, and the prisoner was set free. This time the Burgundians would not do it; they sold Joan to England for a heavy price. Many historians blame Charles VII for making no effort to rescue her. Whether Charles was unable to ransom her, or deliberately chose not to, historians are not of one mind. But one thing is clear: after the woman who had saved his throne was captured, he did not get her out.

Joan was held in a prison in Rouen and tried several times to escape. Once she jumped from a tower more than twenty meters high (some sixty-five feet) and landed in the soft mud of the dry moat, escaping serious injury. But in the end she could not get away.

V. The Trial at Rouen

On January 9, 1431, the trial opened at Rouen. It was presided over by Pierre Cauchon, Bishop of Beauvais, a man aligned with England, whose very standing as her judge was a legal pretense. The conduct of the whole trial, as later ages have put it, was in many respects disorderly and irregular.

The injustice of this trial is written in the record. The notaries were required to collect only testimony unfavorable to Joan; not a word of testimony in her favor was put forward. Joan ought to have been held in a church prison guarded by nuns; instead, the English kept her in an ordinary prison guarded by soldiers. She asked for the Pope to intervene; Cauchon refused, because once the Pope intervened, the trial would have to stop. An illiterate girl of nineteen, with no defender, facing a bench of theologians and judges alone.

The most famous passage in the trial record is the question the judges put to her: "Do you believe yourself to be in the grace of God?" It was a trap: by the doctrine of the time, no one could be certain of being in God's grace — to answer yes was heresy, and to answer no was a confession. Joan replied: "If I am not in it, may God put me there; if I am, may God keep me there." The notary later testified that when the court heard this answer, everyone was stunned, and the questioning for that day had to be suspended.

But the outcome of the trial had been settled long before. In the end the court laid twelve charges against her and, under threat of being put to death on the spot, forced her to sign a public abjuration that she could not read a single word of. A few days later she put men's clothing back on, and the court, on the charge of relapse, sentenced her to the stake. On May 30, 1431, Joan was publicly burned in the Old Market square at Rouen, about nineteen years old. Eyewitnesses said that, bound to the stake, she prayed the whole time, and asked the priest beside her to let her hold a small cross. The English burned her body twice over and scattered the ashes into the Seine, so that no one could collect her remains.

Epilogue: The Reversal, Twenty-Five Years Later

After Joan's death, the Hundred Years' War went on for another twenty-two years. In the 1450s the English were finally driven out of France altogether. Joan's aged mother, Isabella, petitioned the Pope to reopen her daughter's case. In 1456 the Pope ordered a retrial; the court examined the testimony of 115 eyewitnesses, and on July 7 of that year formally declared that the original trial was null and void, and that she was innocent. On May 16, 1920, the Church formally canonized her as a saint.

From the stake to the reversal was only twenty-five years. From the reversal to canonization was 464 years. A farm girl of nineteen saved a country, and never received that country's rescue in return. When she died, France did nothing for her; after her death, France spent six hundred years telling her into a national heroine.

The most painful part of Joan's story is not the burning itself, but the stretch in between: she saved Charles VII, and Charles VII did not save her. That account between a loyal servant and her sovereign was the same six hundred years ago, and reading it six hundred years later, it still chills the heart.

After her death, Joan's image was retold by generation after generation. In the age of Napoleon, French politicians used her for their propaganda; in the Second World War, the Free French led by de Gaulle printed the Cross of Lorraine, from her home region, on their military banners. She went from a farm girl to the symbol of a nation. Everyone who tells her story is, in truth, telling of the France in their own heart. But however the story is told, one thing cannot be changed: she was only nineteen years old, she could not read, she stood alone before the court and answered a question that left every judge stunned — and then she walked calmly to the stake.
