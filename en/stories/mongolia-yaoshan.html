The 1993 "Demon Mountain" Disaster: Seven People Went Into the Mountains, and Only a Seventeen-Year-Old Girl Walked Out

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

First, let us get the place right. The story that circulates online calls this the "Mongolian Demon Mountain disaster," but that mountain is not in the country of Mongolia. It is the Khamar-Daban range, in Russia's Republic of Buryatia, on the southern shore of Lake Baikal. The Buryats are a branch of the Mongolian peoples; they speak a dialect of Mongolian, practice Tibetan Buddhism, and have also kept their shamanic traditions. In the eyes of the local people, these mountains were never ordinary mountains. As for why they came to be called a "demon mountain," that begins with the summer of 1993.

In August 1993, a hiking party set out from Petropavlovsk, in northern Kazakhstan, and rode the train to Murino, a small town on Baikal's southern shore. The leader was Lyudmila Korovina, 41, a Master of Sport of the Soviet Union — a title that carried real weight in the sporting system of the time. Leading students on treks was her trade; she had taken a team across the Tien Shan before. Of the six who died, she was the oldest; the youngest, Timur, was only 15. The party had spent half a year preparing for this trip. The six members were all young people from her hiking club: Tatyana, 24; Alexander, 23; Denis, 19; Valentina, 17; Victoria, 16; and Timur, 15. The youngest was fifteen, and not one of them was a beginner.

The route was not a dangerous one; the highest peak, Mount Khan-Uula, stands 2,371 meters, and in summer there is no ice or snow. The plan was to cross the ridge, descend to Lake Patovo, and meet another party led by Korovina's daughter. That party made its whole way without incident.

In this party, Alexander was a student at Moscow University; Timur came from a mountaineering family; Valentina was studying tourism and sport at a pedagogical university, and in the ordinary course of things she would one day have become a trek leader like Korovina. The first days went reasonably well, except that heavy rain poured down for three days straight — the trail was hard going, the people were worn out, and even lighting a fire was difficult. On August 4, the party passed through the Langutai Pass and climbed into the Mount Khan-Uula area, camping on the mountain that night, in rain so heavy no fire could be lit at all. According to Valentina, on the morning of August 5, Korovina woke everyone, they managed with effort to get a fire going, ate breakfast, and began the descent. The catastrophe happened on that descent, at a place about 2,396 meters up.

As Valentina later told it, Alexander, walking at the rear of the line, suddenly screamed. He was the strongest of the whole party, a hiker since the age of twelve; now he fell to the ground convulsing, blood seeping from his eyes and ears, white foam at his mouth. Korovina ran to help him, and before long she showed the same symptoms herself and collapsed on top of Alexander. Tatyana rushed up to help, then began clawing at her own throat as if she could not breathe, and beat her head against a rock beside her until she lay still. Victoria and Timur turned and ran downhill, and as they ran they too fell, vomiting blood and tearing at their clothes. Denis kicked Valentina and shouted at her to get down the mountain — but in the end he did not come down himself.

Within a few minutes, six of the seven were down.

Valentina hid for a night low on the slope. The next morning she climbed back to the place where it had happened; everyone lay in the same posture as the day before, and not one of them was alive. She took a map and compass from her companions' packs and walked down the mountain alone. She walked in those mountains for four days and four nights; she began to run a high fever, coughed without stopping, and found it harder and harder to breathe. By her own account, she climbed into the icy river at one point, meaning to wash herself clean and then wait there to die. Just then, a party of Ukrainian tourists in a catamaran came around a bend in the river and saw her waving from the bank. The retellings differ a little on the details of her route down — some say she followed the river, some say she followed a power line — but the rescue itself is agreed. As the tourists who saved her later recalled, she threw herself into one team member's arms and cried for a long time, incoherent, repeating only that people had died and that she was frightened. For days afterward, she could not get out one complete sentence.

The facts that can be confirmed are these: on August 5, 1993, six members of the party died. The autopsies, performed in Ulan-Ude, concluded that Korovina had died of cardiac arrest and the other five of hypothermia. The autopsies also found that the dead generally showed signs of malnutrition, with a lack of glycogen in the muscles and liver, and bruising in the lungs; when the searchers found the bodies, three of them had torn their outer clothing open, and two were barefoot. Hypothermia has a phenomenon called "paradoxical undressing": in the final stage of freezing, a person feels a false flush of heat and takes his own clothes off — and that fits the forensic conclusion.

What does not fit is the speed. Hypothermia is a slow process. It does not bring down six strong young people on the same stretch of ridge within a few minutes of one another, accompanied by bleeding and convulsions. The official search, too, was long delayed: Korovina's daughter waited two full days at Lake Patovo, and when her mother's party never came out of the forest, she led her own team down first, reaching Slyudyanka on August 18 and reporting it at once. Because of bad weather, the helicopter did not take off until the 21st, and the search party — led by Yuri Goliyus of the local rescue service — did not begin searching until the 24th. Finding the bodies alone took two days, because Valentina still could not say exactly where it had happened. By then, nearly twenty days had passed since the event. As Goliyus later recalled, the six lay together on an open slope, their brightly colored backpacks visible from far away.

Everything beyond that is conjecture.

One account is malnutrition compounded by extreme weather. In those days the mountains saw torrential rain and low temperatures; the party's provisions were not ample; cold and hungry, their bodies gave out first, and then the hypothermia took them. Goliyus himself mentioned in interviews that the rations Korovina carried were on the low side, a point that has been discussed again and again since. Others have proposed high-altitude pulmonary edema; Valentina herself believed that at the time, and on her way down she did show the symptoms — high fever, dry cough, difficulty breathing. Another account is food poisoning. According to Valentina, along the way Korovina had the group gather a plant called "golden root" — rhodiola — and dry it to carry down the mountain. Some suspect they ate a poisonous plant by mistake; others point the finger at the rhodiola itself. None of these accounts has any hard evidence behind it, and the autopsies returned no conclusion of poisoning. There are wilder guesses still — that they strayed into some test area, that they encountered a nerve agent. Those are pure after-the-fact embroidery, with no evidence at all.

There is one more account, put forward by Yuri of the rescue team himself. He said that the Buryats regard rhodiola as a sacred plant, and that in local belief Khamar-Daban was never a place to be entered lightly — tradition holds that there were altars there as far back as the age of the Huns. Korovina's party gathered the rhodiola by the armful, which in local eyes was an offense. And so the "shaman's curse" spread, and the name "demon mountain" came with it. This part belongs to local faith and later telling; it can be recorded, but it cannot be connected to the cause of six deaths.

After her rescue, Valentina quickly vanished from public view. Where she went and how she lived, no one knew for more than twenty years. Not until 2018, when journalists from Komsomolskaya Pravda found her, did she give her first systematic account of what had happened that year; later she appeared on a television program as well. Counting from 1993, she had been silent for twenty-five or twenty-six years. The details she told — the bleeding, the convulsions, the head beaten against a rock — have never been reconciled with the forensic conclusions, and to this day no one has been able to close that gap.

The affair has therefore acquired a nickname: "Buryatia's Dyatlov Pass." The deaths of the nine hikers at Dyatlov Pass in 1959 likewise left behind an official conclusion and a heap of questions it could not answer. The two records simply lie side by side: one written by forensic doctors — death by hypothermia and heart failure; one spoken by the only person who lived — death came like a gust of wind, and in a few minutes swept down a whole party. Which of the two is closer to the truth, no one can prove.

The mountain is still there. The Buryats still hold rhodiola sacred, and tourists still go into the mountains. What actually happened on that ridge on August 5, 1993, only one person ever saw. What she says, people can neither wholly believe nor wholly disbelieve.
