The "Curse" of Jinan's Shuangru Village: A Qing-Dynasty Stone Stele, and a Han King's Tomb That Was Never Robbed

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~10 min read

Let the most important thing be said first, before the story drifts any further from the truth: Shuangru Village, in Jinan's Changqing District, did not become a deserted village. People still live there, and in 2017 it was entered on the list of China's traditional villages. The version that circulates online — "in 1995 an ancient tomb was dug out, the villagers died or went mad, a curse descended, and the whole village moved away" — takes a true story and bends most of it out of shape. But the true story itself is strange enough.

Shuangru Village stands in Guide Town, Changqing District, Jinan, built around Mount Shuangru. The mountain originally had two summits, east and west, and the villagers lived off it: from the 1960s onward they quarried its stone. By the account of the village elders, a day of quarrying stone could earn fifty or sixty yuan, and over a hundred on a good day; the stone was sold for money and used to build their own houses, and many of the village's houses and yards are built of stone from that mountain. The villagers had originally lived on the plain not far from the Yellow River; the river flooded year after year, and after 1958 they moved, group by group, to the foot of the mountain, in a government-planned relocation, and the village grew up around the hill. That frightening stele was built into the courtyard wall of one household at the time of the move, and it stayed inside that wall for decades.

In October 1995, cultural-relics workers from Changqing, conducting a relics survey in the village, discovered a stone stele in a villager's courtyard wall. The stele had been erected in the Xuantong years of the Qing dynasty, and its characters were still clear. Carved on it was this: "Before the village there has long stood Mount Shuangru. Though it is no famous mountain, the village relies on it for its safety. Wherever its veins run, they concern the village. If anyone quarries the mountain and takes its stone at will, unexpected calamity will come upon the village."

Put into today's words: whoever quarries the mountain and takes its stone, the whole village will suffer for it.

As word of the inscription spread, the villagers grew uneasy. Because at just about that time, a young man in the village named Baoshan had died, only in his thirties. According to the villagers, Baoshan had quarried stone on the mountain in his lifetime, and after coming home had fallen ill and never recovered, gone in less than a year. More striking still, while quarrying, Baoshan had once opened a hole in the mountain. Villagers recall that he found the stone inside the hole different from the stone elsewhere; the matter was reported upward, the authorities concerned came to look, and confirmed that it was the passage of an ancient tomb. The excavation was led by Professor Ren Xianghong of Shandong University, and ran from October 1995 to July 1996. And Baoshan's house faced directly toward the tomb's doorway. Laid out piece by piece, quite a few villagers reached their conclusion: Baoshan had disturbed the ancient tomb, and had met with a curse.

The panic was real. When CCTV's program Walking into Science came to the village to film in 2006, villagers were still worshipping gods and Buddhas and praying for protection, and the program mentioned that a few households, out of fear, had moved their whole families away from the village. But "the whole village moved away" never happened. The program stated it plainly: at that time the village had more than four hundred households and more than sixteen hundred people, living their lives as usual.

The ancient tomb, though, was real, and it carried enormous weight. In fact, as early as the 1970s, the villagers' quarrying had already been bringing this tomb to light bit by bit; no one knew what it was. From October 1995, the Shandong Provincial Cultural Relics Bureau, Shandong University, and the Changqing relics authorities excavated it jointly for eight months, unearthing more than 2,300 objects; it was selected among the Ten Great Archaeological Discoveries in China for 1996. It is the only Han-dynasty royal tomb formally excavated in Shandong since 1949 that had never been robbed. The tomb's occupant was Liu Kuan, the last king of the Kingdom of Jibei, a clansman of the Western Han imperial house and a fifth-generation descendant of Liu Bang. The Kingdom of Jibei was a vassal kingdom established in the second year of Emperor Wen of Han; it passed through several generations, and Liu Kuan's was the last. His tomb and the tomb of his queen, on the western summit, occupy the two peaks, one each; their forms are basically the same, both being vertical rock-cut pit chambers hewn into the mountaintops, the queen's tomb slightly smaller in scale. That is how the name "Shuangru Mountain Han Tombs" came about. The queen's tomb has not been excavated to this day.

The man Liu Kuan himself is recorded in the official histories in terms that are far from flattering. According to the Book of Han, he committed adultery with his father Liu Hu's queen and concubines, and during a sacrifice laid a curse on Emperor Wu of Han; when the matter came to light he killed himself in fear of punishment, in the eleventh year of his reign (Director Zhang of the museum, who took part in the excavation, said twelve years on the program — both versions stand), and the Kingdom of Jibei was abolished in consequence and placed under Taishui Commandery. Interestingly, the archaeology matches the record: in Liu Kuan's tomb there was no gold-threaded jade burial suit of the kind standard for a vassal king, no jade seal, and very few jade objects at all — a king who had died in disgrace, by his own hand, was buried at a lowered grade. His tomb was cut into the limestone body of the mountain, in the shape of the character jia, a vertical rock pit with a sloping tomb passage, the chamber and passage both provided with two-tiered ledges, shallow outside and deep inside, covering an area of about 1,500 square meters, the chamber's deepest point about 22 meters down. Among the more than 2,000 grave goods unearthed were bronzes, jades, ironware, lacquerware, pottery, coins, and chariot-and-horse fittings, with the jade face cover and the jade pillow the most exquisite. The jade face cover is assembled from pieces for the forehead, chin-rest, cheeks, jaw, and ears, and is strikingly lifelike; the jade pillow is composed in three layers of nine jade pieces, three jade boards, two jade tiger-head ornaments, and bamboo slats — an ingenious construction. Part of these objects is now displayed in the Shandong Museum. Only, this tomb was eleven years in the building and still unfinished: the passage and drainage system were never completed, and the tomb door was a makeshift pile of stones. The village also has a saying that Liu Kuan's tomb door faced north, unlike other ancient tombs, pointing straight toward Baoshan's house. The words come from the villagers' mouths; whether they are true cannot be verified, but they circulate widely in the village, and they are one of the ropes that tied Baoshan's death to the ancient tomb.

The capital of the Kingdom of Jibei was called Lucheng; its site lies in what is now the "Luchengwa" area of Changqing, not far from Mount Shuangru. On Mount Fulu, a few kilometers northeast of Shuangru, there is another very large royal tomb, which archaeologists preliminarily judge to be that of Liu Kuan's father, Liu Hu, the fourth King of Jibei. The tombs of two generations of kings of this one family are spread across the hills around Shuangru Village; the villagers quarried stone and farmed their fields, which is to say they lived their days beside royal tombs — only for two thousand years, nobody knew it.

So this tomb does have a connection with a "curse" — except that the curse was the one Liu Kuan laid on the emperor, not one the ancient tomb laid on the villagers. A palace scandal of two thousand years ago, passed down to two thousand years later, had its direction reversed.

And now Baoshan. How did he actually die? The Walking into Science reporter asked his wife to her face, and the answer was three words: leukemia. Baoshan himself never went down into the tomb; he had only, while quarrying, happened upon the mouth of the tomb passage. Of the archaeologists who actually excavated the tomb that year, not one came to any harm; Director Zhang of the Changqing District Museum, who took part in the whole excavation, said in his interview that he had "no feeling of that kind at all."

And that frightening stele was not the tomb owner's warning either. According to Director Liu of the museum, in Qing times the stone of Mount Shuangru had been quarried so excessively that the mountain and its soil and water were damaged, and the authorities, in order to close the mountain, erected this stele. The "curse" in the inscription was the government's device for frightening the villagers — in effect, a prohibition carved in stone. In today's words, it is an environmental-protection stele.

And so several threads were twisted together: the stele's harsh words came first, a young man's death by illness came after, and the emergence of the great tomb added fuel. Baoshan's cause of death was leukemia; the stele was a Qing-dynasty notice closing the mountain; the tomb was a Western Han royal mausoleum. Three things that had nothing to do with one another happened to sit in the same village, and once the times and the order got scrambled, the story changed its flavor.

One more detail is worth setting down. After the rumors of the curse spread, no one dared touch another stone on Mount Shuangru. In the words of Old Cao in the village, "Now, on this mountain, not one person dares move a single stone." A Qing-dynasty mountain-closure stele, together with a young man's death by illness, sealed off the livelihood the whole village had eaten from for decades — an outcome, most likely, that the officials who erected the stele never imagined.

Walk into Shuangru Village today, and your eyes are still full of stone: courtyard walls, house foundations, steps, mostly built of stone quarried from the mountain in those years, and that Xuantong-era mountain-closure stele is built into one of those courtyard walls, where it sat for decades before anyone recognized it. The villagers point at the stone houses and say: the mountain was moved into the village long ago.

Mount Shuangru was quarried for decades; its two summits have by now been mostly quarried away, and looking out from the village today, it is hard to make out the shape of "twin breasts" any longer. Liu Kuan's tomb is still there, with its national key cultural relic protection marker standing by it, and you can walk down the tomb passage all the way into the chamber. The village did not empty. It is the mountain that is nearly gone.
