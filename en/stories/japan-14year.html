The Truth Kicked Loose by One Small Fight: The Killer of a High-School Girl Hid for Fourteen Years, and Fell Because of a Dispute with a Coworker

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~10 min read

In April 2018, in Ube, Yamaguchi Prefecture, Japan, a small incident occurred at a civil-engineering company: an employee got into a dispute with a coworker, and it reached the point of police involvement. The employee taken in for questioning was Manabu Kashima, 35, ordinarily a man of few words, conscientious at work, willing to teach the younger staff anything they didn't understand — by common consent of the company, a thoroughly good man.

Following procedure, the police took his fingerprints and collected samples. When the comparison results came back, the officers handling it froze: the fingerprints and DNA matched, exactly, what had been left at the scene of a murder in Hiroshima fourteen years earlier.

That case Hiroshima police had investigated for fourteen years without ever reaching him. He and the victim lived in two different prefectures, were strangers to each other, and had met face to face only once.

Turn the time back to October 5, 2004, in Hatsukaichi, Hiroshima Prefecture.

Satomi Kitaguchi was 17, a second-year student at Hiroshima Prefectural Hatsukaichi High School. School had midterm examinations that day, so she was let out earlier than usual and got home at about two in the afternoon. The room she lived in was not in the main house but in a separate two-story annex behind it. She told her grandmother and younger sister in the main house that she would sleep for a while first, and asked them to wake her at four.

About an hour later, the people in the main house heard screaming.

When the grandmother and the sister rushed over, Satomi had already fallen in a pool of blood at the door of the annex, and a young man was standing beside her. The man turned and lunged at the 72-year-old grandmother, stabbing her more than a dozen times; she fell, gravely wounded, at one point in critical condition, and was later resuscitated. The sister escaped in the confusion and got help from neighbors. By the time the police arrived, the man was long gone.

Satomi had been stabbed more than a dozen times in the head, chest, and neck, and died of blood loss. She was in her second year of high school; her schoolbag was still in her room.

In fact, a good deal was left at the scene: the killer's fingerprints, nail clippings, and one shoe he dropped as he fled. Both the grandmother and the sister had seen his face, and from their accounts the police drew a composite sketch and released it: about twenty years old, traces of acne on his cheeks, hair standing up, about 165 centimeters tall, solidly built, with long, narrow eyes. By the judgment of the time, this case should not have been hard to solve.

And yet it could not be solved.

The police turned over everyone Satomi had been in contact with in her life — classmates, teachers, every man who had had dealings with her — and checked them one by one; not one matched. Crimes of love, crimes of revenge — every direction was tried, and none of them went anywhere. That shoe, that set of fingerprints, that DNA sample were like three keys with no keyhole: plainly held in the hand, and able to open no door at all.

Satomi's father, Tadashi Kitaguchi, did one thing. He took out the 3 million yen of university money he had saved for his daughter and put all of it up as a reward, asking only for a lead that could find the killer. He also started a blog, named "Satomi — Longing for My Daughter," and updated it almost every day, posting leads, posting the sketch, writing how much he missed his daughter. Besides waiting for word from the police, he never stopped investigating on his own, for fourteen years.

From 2004 to 2018, he wrote for thirteen and a half years.

Inside the Hiroshima police, the case had been given a name: "the thirteen-year unsolved murder of a high-school girl." Year after year went by, the staff of the investigation headquarters changed shift after shift, and the case just sat there — the fingerprints and DNA from the scene on file, the sketch posted on the website, waiting for a person who matched to appear.

Where was the killer during those fourteen years? No one would believe it said out loud: he lived in the neighboring Yamaguchi Prefecture, his household registration never moved, he went to work normally and lived normally.

Manabu Kashima was born in 1983 in Shimonoseki, Yamaguchi Prefecture; he worked at a metal-processing plant in Hagi and lived in a company dormitory. On the morning of October 4, 2004, for the first time since he had entered the trade, he overslept. At the thought of being late, he simply quit his job, got on his small motorcycle, and rode aimlessly in the direction of Tokyo.

On October 5 he reached Hatsukaichi, and on the road he saw Satomi coming home from school.

At the later trial, the course of events as established by the prosecution was this: he followed her home, forced his way into the annex while she was alone in it, intending to assault her; Satomi escaped and resisted; enraged and humiliated, he pulled out the folding knife he carried and stabbed her, again and again. The grandmother came running; he stabbed and wounded her too, and fled.

A man who quit his job for no more than oversleeping, one day later, killed a 17-year-old girl he did not know at all. The two lives had never intersected before, and never did again. For fourteen years the police investigated in the direction of "an acquaintance did it," and the direction had been off from the very beginning.

The trial also disclosed some of his background as he grew up: he was born into a family of complicated relationships, had never gotten along with his father, and had kept things pent up inside; a psychiatric evaluation mentioned a developmental deviation — ordinarily quiet, but once worked up, he would use his hands. These were facts the defense laid out in asking for leniency; in the end the court did not soften on their account.

After the crime he went back to Yamaguchi Prefecture and lived as usual. In fourteen years he changed jobs, and finally settled at the civil-engineering company in Ube. In April 2018 he got into a dispute with a coworker at the company; when Yamaguchi police investigated that assault case, they listed him as a person of interest, took his fingerprints and DNA, and sent them for comparison — and they matched what had been left at the scene of the Hiroshima cold case.

The order of the comparison was this: first his entered fingerprints matched the fingerprints at the scene; the police immediately did a DNA recheck, and that matched too. On April 13, 2018, Hiroshima Prefectural Police arrested Manabu Kashima on suspicion of murder. Freshly arrested, he flatly denied it, but the fingerprints and the DNA were right there, and in the end he admitted it. When the news reached the company where he worked, his coworkers were all stunned: the day before the incident he had come to work as normal; he was introverted, spoke little, helped anyone who needed a hand — a model employee without a fault to pick. That quiet Manabu Kashima, and the man in the sketch of fourteen years ago — the same person?

Tadashi Kitaguchi later told reporters that when the news of the killer's arrest arrived, what went around and around in his head was: where on earth have I seen this man? He thought and thought, and could not remember at all. He also said that the matter was settled, in a way, but the thought "I failed to protect my daughter; I am sorry to her" had clung to him all these years, and probably always would. That blog, written for thirteen and a half years — he is still updating it.

What sticks in the throat even more is the sketch. After the killer was caught, Japanese media put the composite sketch released in 2004 side by side with a photograph of Manabu Kashima; the likeness was startling. This face — the police had drawn it fourteen years earlier and posted it everywhere; only no one had been able to match him to it. A Japanese case commentator, reviewing the matter afterward, said that if investigators at the time had not clung so single-mindedly to the "acquaintance seeking revenge" direction, the sketch might have been put to use much earlier. That is hindsight, and whether it counts is another question; but that for fourteen years the man in the sketch lived in the next prefecture and went to and from work normally every day — that is fact.

In March 2020, the trial opened at the Hiroshima District Court. Manabu Kashima admitted all the facts of the indictment, saying, "There is no mistake." The prosecution demanded life imprisonment, on the ground that in this case there was "no room for a fixed-term sentence." The defense raised his developmental disorder and asked for leniency.

In the reasoning of the judgment, the court spoke heavily: a folding knife of no great lethality could produce wounds like those only because, when he struck, he had not hesitated in the least and had used very great force, one stab after another; his rage had originally been aimed at his failure to carry out the assault, and in the end it was all vented on the victim.

On those days of the trial, Tadashi Kitaguchi sat in the courtroom the whole time, his daughter's portrait in his bag. Fourteen years — he had chased the killer to this point, and now sat a few meters from him, listening to the judge read out the events of that year, one by one.

In his final statement, Manabu Kashima asked the judge for permission to remove his mask; allowed, he turned toward Satomi's parents, seated on the prosecution side, and shouted his apology: he was sorry; it was because of his selfishness that he had taken their family member's life.

On March 18, the sentence came down: life imprisonment, the same as the prosecution had demanded. Neither side appealed, and the judgment became final.

What Satomi's father Tadashi Kitaguchi had wanted all along was the death penalty. After the court adjourned he told reporters that at the moment he heard the main text of the sentence, he had reported it in his heart to his daughter. He said that when he got home he would tell her: "Dad lost."

That blog is still being updated, even after the killer was caught. The thirteen-and-a-half-year wait had its result; but for this father, what the result was — his own sentence has already said it.
