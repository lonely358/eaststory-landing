After the Last Wedding: Where Did Greenland's Vikings Go?

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In September 1408, at the southern end of Greenland, in the church at Hvalsey, a couple was married.

The church was built of stone, a respectable building on that island. The wedding had its witnesses, the document was drawn up as the rules required, and in time it made its way into an archive in Iceland. There is nothing wrong with the document itself. What is wrong with it is that behind it there is nothing at all — it is the last written record the Greenland Norse ever left. After it, only silence.

To understand the people who left that silence, you have to go back more than four hundred years.

Around the year 985, Erik the Red, exiled from Iceland, led a fleet west and made his landing in the fjords of Greenland's southwest coast. According to the sagas, the name "Greenland" — the green land — was one he chose on purpose, because it sounded like a place a man could get through the winter, and because it would tempt people to follow him. The sagas were written down centuries after the events, so that line can only be heard as legend; the migration itself is real. There were two settlements: the Eastern Settlement at the southern end, and, several hundred kilometers to the north, near modern Nuuk, the Western Settlement. The Western Settlement was the smaller, with about ninety farms; the Eastern Settlement was the main body, with its own bishop and cathedral. Even the number of farms in the Eastern Settlement alone is counted by researchers at anywhere from just over a hundred to five hundred. How many Norse Greenlanders there were at the peak, estimates run from two thousand to five thousand — a wide spread, because all anyone can do is count farm ruins and reason backward.

According to the sagas, Erik himself had taken to the sea because he had killed a man in Iceland and been sentenced to exile. Their timing was fortunate: those centuries fell within the Medieval Warm Period, the sailing season through the straits was long, and the grass in the fjords could support cattle and sheep. The first settlers chose their ground carefully: deep in the fjords, sheltered from the wind, with summer pasture, and close to the sea — fish, seals, and walrus in the water, reindeer on land. On that half-sea, half-pasture way of life, they lasted more than four hundred years. By the mid-twelfth century, Greenland had its own bishop, and in European eyes the size of its population and its number of churches made it a proper society.

They lived at the end of the world for four and a half centuries, and not on farming alone. Cattle and sheep had to be raised and hay laid in, but Greenland's truly valuable produce was in the sea: walrus ivory.

In medieval Europe, ivory was hard currency, carved into crosses, chess pieces, staffs, and reliquaries. Supplies of African elephant ivory came and went; North Atlantic walrus ivory filled the gap. In 2018, teams at the University of Cambridge and the University of Oslo analyzed the DNA of walrus ivory objects excavated across Europe and found that after the last ice age the Atlantic walrus had split into eastern and western populations; from the twelfth century onward, the ivory in European workshops was almost entirely from the western population — the one found only in the waters between Canada and Greenland. In other words, for more than two hundred years the Greenlanders held a near-monopoly on Europe's ivory supply, with exports peaking around 1250. Ivory bought the things the island did not produce: timber, iron, grain. The island's churches and its bishop, too, depended on that sea route to stay in touch with the old world.

In 2024, a genetic tracing study published in Science Advances pushed that hunting line a long way further north. The researchers matched the DNA of thirty-one ivory artifacts to specific walrus populations and found that much of the ivory came from High Arctic waters — above all from the North Water polynya, between northwest Greenland and Ellesmere Island, and possibly from deep inside the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. That means the late-period Greenlanders either sailed themselves on expeditions to hunting grounds more than a thousand kilometers away, or traded for tusks with the Thule Inuit who were moving into the Arctic in the same period — very likely both. One explanation the researchers offer is that the walrus nearer home had been hunted down to scarcity, and the hunting grounds had to be pushed ever further out. Medieval writings left a clue: every summer the Greenlanders organized expeditions north to a stretch of water called the Northern Hunting Grounds to hunt walrus. In the early years it was a routine journey; later, the voyages grew longer and the risks greater. The radius of a single trade drew the last boundary of that society's activity.

The hunting grounds pushed further out brought an unavoidable encounter with them. From the thirteenth century, the Thule people — the ancestors of today's Inuit — migrated east from the direction of Alaska, into the Canadian Arctic and northern Greenland. They were professional sea hunters, every bit the Norse's equals at taking walrus. Sooner or later the two peoples met on the same hunting grounds. The sagas preserve records of clashes with people called Skrælings, and archaeology can trace Thule seasonal camps moving steadily south, reaching coasts not far from the Eastern Settlement. But it has to be said plainly: there is no archaeological evidence that the Norse were driven out or wiped out by the Thule. The meeting is real; trade very likely happened; a war of extermination has no evidence behind it.

Then there is the most widely told old story of all: that the Vikings stubbornly insisted on being European farmers, clung to their cattle and sheep, refused to learn from the Inuit and eat seal, and in the end froze and starved. That version has been overturned by their own bones.

Teams from Denmark and Canada analyzed the carbon isotopes in the bones of eighty Norse settlers. The results showed that in the first years after landing, only twenty or thirty percent of their food came from the sea; by the fourteenth century, seal made up fifty to eighty percent of the diet. They ate seal as expertly as their neighbors did; it was only in their sense of identity that they still thought of themselves as farmers. Jette Arneborg, an archaeologist at the National Museum of Denmark, has put it this way: "farmer" was more a matter of imagined identity, upheld by an upper layer whose standing rested on land and livestock; ordinary people in fact ate whatever there was, without fuss. Niels Lynnerup of the University of Copenhagen, who took part in the research, was blunter: there is no sign whatsoever that they died in some natural catastrophe; if anything, it looks as though they slowly left.

The bones hold another clue. In the late-period cemeteries, the graves of young women are conspicuously few. Researchers infer from this that the young left first — saved up the fare and took ship back to Iceland or Norway. With women of childbearing age steadily draining away, the population that remained could not sustain itself. The Western Settlement emptied first: radiocarbon dating shows it was abandoned around 1400. The Eastern Settlement held on until about 1450. There are no traces of massacre, no burned farms. The frozen-earth cemetery at Herjolfsnes, at the southern end, makes the point best: in 1921, the Danish archaeologist Poul Nørlund excavated there a batch of remarkably preserved woolen burial clothes, cut in exactly the fashions current in Europe at the time — even the hats were in the Burgundian style fashionable in the fifteenth century. From this, Nørlund inferred that the Greenlanders may have lived on until around 1480; but that late date rests on clothing styles alone, the evidence is thin, and most scholars still place the end around 1450. The animal-bone record shows the same thing: they kept fewer and fewer livestock — not because they clung to their farms to the death, but because the farms, one after another, failed first. They stayed in contact with the old world to the very end; it was only that the ships came less and less often.

Why they left, scholarship has no single answer for; the factors on the table are a pile: the climate turned into the Little Ice Age, sea ice thickened, the sea routes grew dangerous; the ivory trade declined, as both demand and supply in Europe changed; Norway itself, after the Black Death, had troubles enough of its own, and sailings fell away sharply; the hunting grounds stretched ever further while able-bodied hands ran short. And there is one more thing that does not survive arithmetic: there were too few people. A society of a few thousand at its peak, scattered across two settlements and several hundred farms — one shipwreck, one bad year, and the proportional loss is frightening. A society like that has no redundancy; it cannot absorb a single heavy blow. Which factor was the main one has been argued for decades, and the argument goes on. What is certain is that they adapted — the isotope record of their diet is there to prove it — and that they failed; only the manner of the failure was not a famine, but a long evacuation.

So the word "disappearance" is, in fact, the wrong word. The likelier process is this: household by household, they moved away — the young first, then whole families; farms stood empty, churches went unattended, and the last people closed their doors and boarded a ship. A few decades after that wedding in 1408, the written record does not contain one more word. Nobody recorded the ending, because the ending was not an event — it was a society taking several decades to slowly empty itself out.
