Who Put Bella in the Wych Elm?

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~8 min read

On April 18, 1943, in Hagley Wood in Worcestershire, England, four local boys were roaming the woods. Ostensibly they were out looking for birds' nests; in fact they were also doing a bit of poaching on the side — the wood belonged to Lord Cobham's estate, and outsiders were not allowed in.

One of the boys, Bob Farmer, climbed a large wych elm. Partway up the trunk there was a hollow; he peered in and saw a skull. At first he thought it was an animal's; then he looked again — there was hair, there were teeth: it was human. The other three boys — Robert Hart, Thomas Willetts, and Fred Payne — were waiting below; none of them had expected that poking around for birds' nests would turn up something like this. He put the skull back where it was, and the four boys went home without a word — they knew perfectly well they had been standing where they should not have been.

But once home, the eldest, Thomas Willetts, could not sit still, and told his parents. The police then entered the wood.

The Woman in the Hollow Tree

Inside the hollow trunk the police found a nearly complete skeleton. She wore a striped sweater and a mustard-yellow skirt; on her feet were size five-and-a-half blue shoes with crepe soles, and among the remains was a gold wedding ring. Some distance from the tree, they also found the bones of a hand — buried separately in the earth.

The remains were sent to James Webster, a forensic pathologist in Birmingham. His conclusion: the dead woman had been dead at least 18 months, which meant she had died in or before October 1941; she was between 25 and 40 years old, and only five feet tall, about 1.52 meters. In her mouth was a piece of taffeta, on the basis of which he judged the cause of death to be suffocation. Webster also measured the hollow of the tree and inferred that the body had been forced in while it was still "warm," before rigor mortis set in — once the corpse had stiffened, that opening could never have admitted an adult body.

The coroner's jury at the inquest returned a verdict of "murder by person or persons unknown," and nothing more. Then came the hardest step: who was she? The police actually had a good deal to work with — her appearance could be roughly reconstructed, her teeth were highly distinctive, and the shoes were a style manufactured only after 1940. They checked her features against missing-person records across the whole region, printed and distributed notices describing her clothing and teeth, and contacted dentists everywhere. But 1943 was the fiercest stage of the Second World War; there were far too many missing people, the records piled up mountain-high, and not one of them matched, nor did anyone come forward to claim her. The inquest report later went missing, and the skeleton itself, too, vanished to parts unknown.

The Writing on the Wall

About eight months after the body was found, in 1944, graffiti began to appear nearby.

The earliest sentence was written on a wall on Reservoir Road in Old Hill, Halesowen, in chalk; the police believed it had been written before Christmas 1943. It read: "Who put Lubella down the wych elm?" In March 1944, a second appeared on a derelict building in Upper Dean Street, Birmingham: "Who put Bella down the Wych Elm — Hagley Wood?" Two days later, another turned up on nearby Pershore Street: "Hagley Wood Bella." All of these writings were placed high up, not where a child could have climbed to write them. The police compared the handwriting and concluded it was the work of the same person. The archaic spelling "wych" also caught police attention — they guessed the writer might be a country person familiar with the local markets.

The crux is the name. At the time, the police had never announced any name for the dead woman, because they did not know it themselves. "Bella" was a name the graffiti gave her. In 1944, a sex worker operating around Hagley Road in Birmingham reported to the police that among her colleagues there had been a woman called Bella, who had gone missing about three years earlier. That lead was never followed to any result, but it at least suggested a possibility: whoever wrote the graffiti might really have known who the dead woman was.

From the 1970s onward, the sentence "Who put Bella in the wych elm" began appearing again and again on the Hagley Obelisk — the monument not far from where the body was found. It appeared once more in 1999, with the spelling now changed to "witch elm." In 2020, the graffiti was altered again. The sentence has been written for eighty years, and the question is still the same question.

Several Theories, None of Them Landed

Over eighty-odd years, several accounts have arisen of who Bella was and why she died. By the rules, they are set side by side here; not one of them has been confirmed.

One account comes from 1953. Una Mossop stated to the police that her late husband, Jack Mossop, had confessed to his family that he and a Dutchman, van Raalte, had put a woman into the hollow tree. By this statement, the two men had been drinking at the Lyttelton Arms pub in Hagley with a Dutch woman in their company; she drank too much and passed out in the car, and the two put her into a hollow tree trunk in the woods, meaning for her to get a fright when she woke in the morning and learn her lesson. Jack Mossop afterward, tormented by recurring dreams of a woman staring at him from inside a tree, was committed to a mental hospital in Stafford, and died there before the body was ever found. The problem with this statement is obvious: Una spoke only more than a decade after Jack's death, and its contents cannot be checked.

One account involves spies. MI5 declassified a file on Josef Jakobs — the last man executed at the Tower of London, put to death on August 15, 1941. This German agent had parachuted into Cambridgeshire, broken his ankle on landing, and been captured almost at once. On him was a photograph said to be of his lover, the German cabaret singer Clara Bäuerle. Jakobs said she, too, was training as a spy and might have been sent to make contact with him inside Britain. So some guessed that the woman in the elm was Bäuerle. That line was later closed off: there is no evidence Bäuerle ever came to Britain; people who knew her said she stood nearly six feet tall, while Bella was five feet; and in 2016 it was established that Bäuerle had died in Berlin on December 16, 1942.

Another account, also from 1953, holds that the dead woman was a Dutchwoman named Clarabella Drönke, silenced by a German spy ring composed of a British officer, Dutchmen, and music-hall performers because she "knew too much." The surviving records cannot support this story.

The last comes from an anthropologist. In 1945, Margaret Murray of University College London proposed that this was a ritual witchcraft killing: the separately buried hand, in her view, matched a rite known as the "Hand of Glory." The theory caused a sensation in the local papers and even led investigators for a time to connect it with another, seemingly ritual-flavored murder nearby. But it, too, remained conjecture, with no evidence to follow it up.

Eighty Years On, They Are Still Looking for Her Bones

West Mercia Police reviewed the case in 2014; it still produced no new leads. In 2018, the Face Lab at Liverpool John Moores University made a facial reconstruction from photographs of the skull, and her appearance was made public in image form for the first time — the ordinary face of a middle-aged woman, with nothing distinctive enough for anyone to recognize her by.

More troublesome still is the physical evidence. Until the late 1960s or early 1970s, her remains were kept in a "black museum" at a Birmingham City Police training center; after that, their whereabouts are unknown. Without remains, no DNA test can be done. In May 2023, the BBC made a dedicated appeal, asking museums to help search for the remains so that her DNA could be analyzed. After the appeal went out, the bones still had not been found.

The case has also been carried again and again onto the stage and the page: in 2003 someone made it into a chamber opera; in 2018 Tana French published the novel *The Witch Elm*; and a musical of the same name has been staged in London. Every adaptation uses the name from the graffiti.

So what remains of this case today is: a sentence of graffiti written for eighty years, a name the graffiti gave her, and an elm tree that no longer exists. Her own name, to this day, nobody knows.
