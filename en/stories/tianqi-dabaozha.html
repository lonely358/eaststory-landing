The Blast That Flattened Half of Beijing: The Tianqi Explosion of 1626

Strange Cases · Nov 21, 2026 · ~10 min read

On May 30, 1626, the day after the Dragon Boat Festival, at nine in the morning, a single roar tore out of the Wang Gongchang arsenal in the southwest of Beijing, and half the capital caved in. The Tianqi Emperor was having breakfast in the Palace of Heavenly Purity. He dropped his bowl and ran. A eunuch beside him was struck on the head by a falling roof tile and killed on the spot. In the emperor's arms was his third son, a baby a few months old. The fright was too much for the child; he died that same day.

Nearly four hundred years have passed, and to this day no one can say what it was that actually exploded. A fire in the powder magazine, a meteorite, an earthquake, a tornado — four explanations have been argued for four centuries, and none has ever persuaded the others. Stranger still, the blast left behind a set of eerie phenomena at the scene that not one of the four theories can explain.

I. What Actually Happened That Morning

The Wang Gongchang was the Imperial Board of Works' gunpowder factory and storehouse. It stood in what is now the Yongning Hutong area of Beijing's Xicheng District, and it held the gunpowder used by the Three Great Camps of the capital garrison. Between seventy and eighty men were stationed there, and every five days the Three Great Camps drew more than 3,000 jin of powder from it (a jin is about 0.5 kg, so roughly 1.5 metric tons). The Ming court maintained six such powder works in the capital; the Wang Gongchang was the largest of them. What blew up, in other words, was no small workshop. It was the central magazine for the entire capital's firearms supply. At about nine o'clock on the morning of May 30, 1626, there first came a roar, like something bellowing, rolling in from the northeast. Grey vapors surged up, the houses began to sway, and then came the great shock — and the sky went black.

According to the *Tianbian Dichao*, the court report on the disaster drawn from the official gazette, the devastated zone ran east to the Xuanwu Gate and west to the Fucheng Gate, three to four li long (a li is about 0.5 km) and thirteen li around, with every house ground to powder. In modern terms, the blast radius was about 750 meters and the affected area about 2.25 square kilometers. On Shifuma Street, a great stone lion weighing 5,000 jin (about 2.5 metric tons) was hurled clean outside the Xuanwu Gate. Heavy timbers flew dozens of li away, all the way to Miyun. Clothing, silver, and jewelry were sucked up into the sky and came down on the trees of the Western Hills, while the drill ground at Changping was left littered with debris.

The Forbidden City did not escape either. The Tianqi Emperor was at breakfast in the Palace of Heavenly Purity when the palace shook and the ground heaved; he rose and ran for the Hall of Union and Peace with a single attendant at his side, and halfway there the attendant was struck in the head by a flying tile and died where he fell. Two thousand workmen then repairing the great halls were shaken from the scaffolding and dashed to their deaths. The elephant stables collapsed, and the panicked elephants broke loose and rampaged through the streets, trampling still more people to death or injury.

II. How Many Died? The Numbers Don't Agree

How many people this explosion killed is a question the sources answer with two very different numbers.

The *Veritable Records of Emperor Xizong of Ming* gives the result of the official investigation: more than 10,930 houses collapsed, and 537 men and women were crushed to death. That figure came from Li Canran, the censor for the western city, who went through the ruins house by house with his men; only the dead who could be named and identified were counted.

The *Tianbian Dichao* gives another number altogether: houses in the tens of thousands, people in the tens of thousands. By its account, more than 20,000 died. A court report was written for the emperor and the throne to read, and there were reasons to write the number large: it made the calamity look more terrifying, and it served the old purpose of using a disaster from Heaven to admonish the emperor.

Which of the two numbers should we believe? The official 537 is certainly an undercount. Families wiped out entirely, with no one left to claim the bodies, never appeared in the investigation at all; neither did those who had fled the city or whose identities were unknown. And the report's twenty thousand is just as certainly rounded upward. All this account can honestly do is set the two figures side by side — 537 by the official investigation, more than 20,000 by the court report — and leave the reader to judge which is closer to the truth.

III. Three Mysteries No One Has Explained to This Day

What truly keeps people awake over this explosion is the three strange phenomena left at the scene.

The first: the dead were all naked. Men and women alike, the dead and injured were stripped of every stitch of clothing, their bodies bare. Some say the blast wave tore the clothes off them. Others say later generations embellished the story until it grew stranger with every retelling. Neither claim can be proven, and neither can be disproven; for now it can only be recorded as it stands.

The second: not a splinter of wood was burned. The Wang Gongchang was a gunpowder storehouse, yet at the center of the explosion there were no traces of burning. The *Zhuozhong Zhi*, a memoir of palace life, states it plainly: in the flattened houses, the fires in the stoves had all gone out; only the two or three wooden rooms of Zhang Si, a wine seller, caught fire, and nothing else burned. The powder exploded, and yet the fires went out. On its face, it makes no sense.

The third: the stone lion that flew out through the city gate. That 5,000-jin lion on Shifuma Street was flung outside the Xuanwu Gate, yet the city wall was not damaged at all. What force could throw a three-ton stone clean out of a city gate and not harm a hair on the wall? Four hundred years on, no one has answered that.

IV. Four Theories, Four Hundred Years of Argument

There is still no settled answer to what caused it. Four theories dominate, and each explains only part of the evidence.

The gunpowder theory is the most direct: the Wang Gongchang was a powder storehouse, the powder went up, and Beijing paid the price. But black powder is a slow-burning, low-order explosive; it is hard to see how it could release so much energy in a single instant, and the theory explains neither the unburned wood nor the flying lion. Powder works had been managed in the Ming capital for well over a century; if one was going to go, it should have burned magazine by magazine, not detonated across the whole compound at once.

The meteorite airburst theory explains the most. First there was a fireball in the sky and a roar, then a smoke column like a mushroom cloud shot upward; around the Xi'an Gate, iron slag fell from the sky, fine as bran. The Tunguska explosion of 1908 went off in just this way. But the theory is missing one crucial piece of evidence: an impact crater. Satellite images show a ring-shaped dark patch in the Lotus Pond area, which some take to be the scar of an impact — but that is far from enough.

The earthquake theory has its supporters too: the rumbling earth, the shaking palaces, a roar heard hundreds of li away all resemble an earthquake. But the stricken zone was so small and the destruction so concentrated, while the nearby Zhenru Temple and Cheng'en Temple suffered little damage — a pattern almost unheard of in the history of earthquakes. And an earthquake explains neither the mushroom cloud nor the naked dead.

The tornado theory accounts for objects being carried up into the sky and for the sharp boundary between the ruined zone and the untouched streets beyond it. But the sound was heard in Tongzhou, Miyun, and Changping, and as far away as Datong, hundreds of li off — no tornado can do that.

Four theories, four centuries of argument, and not one of them can account for every phenomenon at a single stroke. That is what makes the Tianqi Explosion so singular: the more detailed the historical record, the less it can be explained.

V. After the Blast, the Court Busied Itself with Confessing Fault

When news of the explosion spread, court and capital were shaken. Officials submitted memorial after memorial declaring that this was Heaven's warning to the emperor. The Tianqi Emperor issued an edict of self-reproach, declaring that he would reflect deeply on his failings, and released 10,000 taels of gold from the imperial treasury for disaster relief.

But there is another layer here, and it deserves a wary eye. The *Tianbian Dichao* was written at the height of the eunuch Wei Zhongxian's power. When the report used the calamity to make its point, it carried at least some intention of borrowing Heaven's portent to admonish the emperor, and its descriptions of the strange phenomena cannot all be read as faithful record. In 2009, the Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology convened a symposium devoted to this event; the consensus of those present was that the disaster was not the product of any single cause, and that parts of the *Tianbian Dichao* may not be trustworthy.

One more thing is seldom mentioned: the Wang Gongchang did not explode only once. On the second day of the sixth lunar month in 1638, the same works — by then renamed the Anmin Factory — blew up again. More than ten thousand residents were killed or injured; Wang Fu, the eunuch attached to the factory, and Zhang Zhixiu, the official in charge, were both killed in the blast, and the imperial arsenal was nearly emptied. Some have suspected arson in both explosions, but there is no evidence. Twice in twelve years, and with that, Ming gunpowder production was effectively crippled. That a dynasty's arsenal should explode twice in twelve years is, in itself, a fact worth pondering.

Epilogue: Four Hundred Years On, the Answer Is Still in the Sky

What the Tianqi Explosion left to later generations is not the record of a disaster but a problem that remains unsolved to this day. The time, the place, the number of houses flattened, the competing tallies of the dead — all of these can be checked in the records. The naked dead, the unburned wood, the lion flung out through the gate — all of these stand written in black and white in the sources. Yet fit the pieces together, and they will not form a complete answer.

Perhaps there never was a single answer. Gunpowder, meteorite, earthquake, tornado — any one of them might be true, or the truth of that morning may be all four of them combined. Four hundred years have passed, and what that roar actually was is still waiting to be known. History can record how many died that day and how many houses fell. What it cannot record is where that roar came from.

Go today to the Yongning Hutong area of Xicheng District, and there is nothing left to see. The ground where it happened was long ago built over by later houses; only those few lines in the histories remain. The roar that tore through the southwest of Beijing at nine o'clock on the morning of May 30, 1626, echoes in the written record still.
