The Picts: The People Who Beat Back Rome's Legions, Then Vanished

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~8 min read

In AD 297, the Roman orator Eumenius, in a speech, wrote down the word "Picts" for the first time. In Latin it is *Picti* — "the painted ones." The Romans believed the unconquerable tribes of the north had a custom of painting and tattooing their bodies, and simply made that their name.

Some scholars see it differently. One researcher has proposed that the word may come from the people's own language, related to *Pecht* in Scots, meaning "the ancestors." Neither account has been settled to this day; what is certain is that "Picts" was a name outsiders started using first. Their neighbors called them by other names entirely: the Irish called them the Cruithni, the Welsh called them the Prydyn. Bede, at the opening of his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People*, counts the languages of Britain — five in all, with Pictish among them, alongside English, British, Irish, and Latin.

Before the word "Picts" appeared, the Romans called the northern tribes Caledonians — which was actually the name of just one tribe, borrowed by the Romans to stand for them all.

Nor were they one tidy people. "Picts" is a collective name for a group of tribes in northern and eastern Scotland, each going its own way in ordinary times, raiding one another over cattle and land, and raising up a common leader only when an outside enemy came. Gordon Noble, an archaeologist at the University of Aberdeen, holds that their ancestors were simply the people who had always lived in that place, not invaders migrated from anywhere. The medieval monk Bede tells an origin story: the Picts sailed from Scythia to Ireland, then crossed to northern Britain, and, lacking women, asked the Irish for wives, who set the condition that the kingship should pass through the female line. Accounts of this sort are today mostly treated as myth.

The Romans dealt with them for over three hundred years and never got the better of it.

In AD 83 or 84, the governor Agricola led his army north and fought a coalition of the northern tribes at Mons Graupius. The man who wrote the battle down was Tacitus, Agricola's son-in-law. He says the Roman legions won a great victory and ten thousand northerners died. The number should be taken with a discount; but even granting him his victory, Rome never gained a footing in the north. In AD 122, the emperor Hadrian ordered a wall built across northern England, some 73 miles long. Twenty years later the line was pushed north and the Antonine Wall built — and abandoned within a generation. Wherever the wall was built, there the empire's frontier retreated to. In AD 367, the Picts, joining with the Scots and the Saxons, swept south and turned Roman Britain upside down. When Rome withdrew from Britain around AD 410, the Picts were still living north of the wall, exactly as they had three hundred years before.

They could neither be beaten nor bought off, and Roman knowledge of them always remained one layer removed. What we know of the Picts today rests mainly on the stones they carved themselves.

Scattered across the fields, churches, and roadsides of eastern and northern Scotland lie several hundred Pictish stones. A set of symbols recurs on them; scholars count some thirty to fifty: a crescent paired with a V-rod, a double disc paired with a Z-rod, and snakes, salmon, eagles, mirrors and combs, and a four-legged beast with a long curling snout, known as the "Pictish Beast," for which no corresponding animal exists in the real world. The same set of symbols, from Orkney to the Firth of Forth, was carved with a high degree of consistency over several centuries — evidence that this was a system of signs recognized across the whole of Pictish society. But what it records, no one can read. Some scholars guess names and genealogies, others guess markers of territory and marriage; none of it is settled. They carved their words in stone, and the stones have survived to today, but the words can no longer be understood.

Scholars divide these stones into three classes. The first are roughly unworked standing stones, carved with symbols only — the earliest, around the seventh century. The second are cross-slabs in relief, a great cross on one face and the old symbols still on the other, mostly eighth century. The third carry Christian subjects only; the symbols are gone. Laid in sequence, the three classes are a compressed history of the Picts: the old signs first coexisted with the cross, and were at last replaced by it. Around Aberlemno in Angus, no fewer than nine or ten such stones are concentrated within a small area.

At their strongest, the Picts could reshape the whole pattern of Britain. In AD 563, the missionary Columba came from Ireland to found a monastery on the island of Iona, then traveled north to meet the Pictish king Bridei I. Columba's biography records that he needed an interpreter to preach at the royal court — a sign that Pictish and Gaelic were not mutually intelligible. The biography also tells how Columba dueled with the Picts' druids at court, in a passage plainly modeled on the story of Moses against Pharaoh's priests in Exodus: it is the church writer's craft, and how much is true and how much invented cannot be separated. Nor was conversion the work of a single night: in AD 617, the monk Donnán and more than fifty companions were killed on a small island, a debt tradition lays at the Picts' door; the old faith retreated slowly.

The biography records one more thing: a water monster in the River Ness was harming people, and Columba made the sign of the cross and drove it off. This is regarded as the earliest source of the Loch Ness Monster legend. It is legend, not historical fact, and is best kept separate.

On May 20, AD 685, at the Battle of Dun Nechtain, the Pictish king Bridei III laid an ambush by a marshy lake, drew King Ecgfrith of Northumbria into the killing ground, and annihilated him along with his army. Northumbria was then the strongest kingdom in Britain; after this battle, its northward expansion stopped. Bede wrote that from that time "the hopes and strength of the English kingdom began to ebb." At Aberlemno in Angus, in the churchyard, stands a Pictish stone whose back is carved with an entire cavalry battle: the riders in nasal helmets are Northumbrians, the long-haired ones Picts. Some scholars believe it depicts the Battle of Dun Nechtain; others dispute even where the battlefield was. What this stone refers to remains undecided to this day.

A people like this was gone from the record by the tenth century. In the first hundred years after the name disappeared, later generations invented all manner of deaths for them: exterminated by the Scots, wiped out in a banquet massacre. The most famous is "MacAlpin's Treason": the tale that Kenneth MacAlpin, king of the Gaels, invited the Pictish nobles to a feast, rigged the benches beneath them, and caught them all in one net. The story comes from legends of the eleventh and twelfth centuries and does not square with the records of the time: contemporary chronicles style Kenneth MacAlpin with the title "King of the Picts."

The real process was nothing so dramatic. One scholar has even advanced a more subversive view: the historian James E. Fraser argues that "the Picts" as a unified self-designation was widely accepted only at the end of the seventh century, under the hegemony of the kingdom of Fortriu. On that account, the Picts first coalesced into a political whole — and not long after, merged into the next one. From the end of the eighth century, Vikings began raiding the northern coasts, and the Pictish and Gaelic kingdoms took the blows together; banding together became the way to survive. The two peoples had been neighbors for generations in any case, with intermarriage among the nobility and overlapping kingship. When Kenneth MacAlpin came to the throne around 843, it was less a conquest than the beginning of a united kingdom. Over the following decades, Gaelic language and Gaelic church culture slowly gained the upper hand. By around 900, the Irish chronicles no longer speak of a "kingdom of the Picts," calling the country instead "Alba," and Donald II, who died in 900, is the first ruler recorded as "King of Alba." The Pictish language gradually fell out of use over the ninth and tenth centuries, and no new symbol stones were carved.

Their own king lists survive in no original; they came down through medieval copies, and the kings in the first half of the list are a mixture of real and invented that cannot be sorted out.

The people did not go; the name went. The Picts' descendants still live on the same land; later they acquired a shared name, and were called Scots. Two centuries more, and even the Scots themselves could no longer say what the Picts had been: twelfth-century Norwegian historians wrote them up as a people barely taller than dwarfs, hiding underground by day. What they left above ground is only those stones no one can read, still standing in Scotland's fields and beside its churches.
