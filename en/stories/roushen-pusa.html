The Incorruptible Monks: A Master Sat in a Vat for Three Years, and Came Out Looking Alive

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~10 min read

In the tenth year of the Zhenyuan era of the Tang dynasty, the year 794, on Mount Jiuhua, a 99-year-old monk gathered his disciples to say farewell, sat down cross-legged, and died, just like that. His name was Jin Qiaojue. Born into the royal house of Silla, he had taken the tonsure at 24, crossed the sea to Tang China with a white dog, and wandered the famous mountains in search of the Way before settling at last on Mount Jiuhua, where he lived in a rock cave, drinking spring water when thirsty and eating a kind of kaolin clay called "white earth" when hungry. According to the temple records and later accounts, three years afterward the monks opened the stone casket holding his remains and found his face as if alive, his hair and nails still there, his joints cracking when the body was moved, with a sound like a golden chain. The disciples concluded that this was Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva come again in the flesh, and enshrined him. From then on, Mount Jiuhua became the sacred seat of Ksitigarbha.

Legend has it that after he was installed in a three-story stone stupa, the tower gave off "divine light and strange colors" at night, and that ridge was afterward called Divine Light Ridge. This account likewise comes from temple records and folk telling, with no corroboration.

This was the beginning of Mount Jiuhua's incorruptible monks. Over the next thousand years and more, the mountain produced over a dozen of them. According to China News Service, Mount Jiuhua has fourteen verifiable cases — among the most anywhere in the country. And yet Mount Jiuhua's climate is damp, with a third of the days in a year wrapped in rain and mist — by all common sense, precisely the environment in which a corpse decays most easily. Nor is Jiuhua the only place with flesh-body Buddhas: Fujian, Shanxi, Guangdong, Hunan, and Henan all have them. In 2015, a museum in Hungary exhibited a thousand-year-old Buddha statue containing the remains of a monk, suspected to be the "Patriarch Zhanggong" stolen years earlier from Datian County in Fujian, and the affair caused quite a stir at the time. A dead man, his body neither buried nor burned, neither rotted nor ruined, pasted with gold leaf and enshrined in a temple — what is actually going on here? The two sides tell completely different stories.

Mount Jiuhua once had an old rule: when a monk passed away, his body was seated cross-legged into a pottery vat, the vat filled with charcoal and the like, sealed and kept for seven days, and then cremated. A very few monks who had left special instructions before death were never taken out; the vat stayed sealed. Three years later, when the vat was opened, now and then the man inside would be found with his face as if alive and his flesh undecayed — and only then was he enshrined. In other words, an incorruptible monk was not a goal to be "made"; the enshrinement came only because the body had first failed to decay. Wan Jiaxiang, head of the religious affairs section of the Mount Jiuhua administrative office, once witnessed a vat-opening himself, and CCTV's *Legends of Eminent Monks* kept the footage: a monk named Puwen, whose vat was opened three years after his death, his body incorrupt — and, the program said, his hair had grown two more inches. This detail is a phenomenon recorded by the program; to this day there is no scientific explanation for it, while devotees take it as one more proof of perfected practice.

Take the most famous case first. The Chan master Wuxia of the Ming dynasty, whose secular name was Haiyu, took the tonsure at 24 on Mount Wutai and later made his way to Mount Jiuhua, where he built a hut and practiced austerities beneath the Zhaixing Rock on Motian Ridge. Few people lived on the mountain and there was nowhere to beg alms, so he drank stream water when thirsty and ate a plant called huangjing when hungry, living in a small stone cave. He did something almost impossible to imagine: he pricked his tongue for blood, mixed it with gold powder, and copied out the *Avatamsaka Sutra* — the *Flower Garland Sutra* — stroke by stroke. According to CCTV's *Legends of Eminent Monks*, he drew blood once every twenty days, and over 28 years completed the whole work: 81 volumes, 420,000 characters. That blood sutra is today a national first-grade cultural relic. In the third year of Tianqi, 1623, Wuxia passed away; the temple stele says he lived to 126. Before the end he left a poem: "An old man's frame, a hundred years and more; the phantom body gaunt, the dharma body full. If a guest asks where I am going — when winter ends and spring returns, the plum will bloom again." Then he sealed the cave mouth with stones and departed without a sound. After his death he was laid into a vat, and three years later, when it was opened, the records say his body was intact and his face as if alive. When the Chongzhen Emperor heard of it, he ordered the building of the Baisui Palace — the Palace of the Hundred Years — had the body gilded and enshrined, and conferred on him the title "Incarnate Bodhisattva." A story still circulates in the Baisui Palace: in the early Qing, when the temple caught fire, the bodhisattva's arms rose of themselves in a gesture of shielding the flames, a great rain came down and put the fire out, and those hands have never come down since. This is a tale passed by word of mouth among devotees, with no other corroboration; recorded here, it can only count as legend.

Now a case much closer to our own time. Master Mingjing, from Langxi in Anhui, was born in 1928 and took the tonsure in 1984 at Tiantai Temple on Mount Jiuhua. He died on September 6, 1992, leaving word before the end: his body was not to be cremated; six years later he would achieve a true body. His disciple Xinlan kept watch over the pottery vat holding the remains for six years. When it was opened in 1998, according to the CCTV program's record, Mingjing's face was as if alive and his body wholly intact — the fourteenth incorruptible monk in Mount Jiuhua's records.

The work of laying a body into a vat is, on Mount Jiuhua, a trade of its own. The CCTV program mentioned a sedan-chair bearer named Jiao Xiaodi, who besides carrying chairs had an ancestral sideline: laying deceased monks into their vats. In the program's footage, people filled the vat with paper packets one by one; locals said the packets held fillers such as charcoal and sandalwood — not medicines.

The craftsman who later took up the trade is an old artisan named Zhu Guosheng. In 2015, China News Service interviewed him; he had handled several cases since the 1990s. By his account, the procedure has just three steps: seating the vat, sealing the vat, opening the vat. After a monk dies, he is seated cross-legged into a large vat; a layer of lime is spread at the bottom, covered with a layer of charcoal; the body is laid in seated; then crushed charcoal and sandalwood are packed around it, and finally the vat's mouth is sealed. Wait about three and a half years and open it: if the face is as if alive and the flesh undecayed, the body is wrapped in gauze, coated with a layer of raw lacquer, and after the lacquer dries, dusted with lime and gypsum powder, and at last pasted with gold leaf to make a golden body. He also said something crucial: the whole process sounds simple, but people who learn it keep failing; the same method used on different people sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. One peculiarity of Jiuhua's flesh bodies is that the internal organs are not removed; from vat-seating to vat-opening, the remains are never touched.

The devotees' explanation operates on another level. In Buddhist teaching, relics are divided into whole-body and fragmented; an undecayed body is called a "whole-body relic," rarer than fragmented relics and taken as proof of perfected practice, something no ordinary person can achieve. It is reported that many monks, before death, stop eating or eat very little, purifying the stomach and intestines in readiness for the final moment. That the body remains behind, undecayed, is in the devotees' eyes a testimony meant for the world to see.

The scientific explanation is much plainer. Mei Quanxi, vice-chairman of the pharmaceutical history branch of the Chinese Pharmaceutical Association, said in an interview that he knows of no Chinese medicinal herb that, eaten in life, could keep a corpse from decaying after death — though in ancient times there were precedents for soaking corpses in medicated liquid after death as a preservative. By his analysis, the principle lies at the physical level: the key to preserving a corpse is dehydration and isolation. The lime and charcoal at the bottom of the vat drew the moisture out of the remains; sandalwood is itself an effective preservative and fixative; the vat was sealed, shutting out air, moisture, and microorganisms; and with the stomach and intestines emptied before death, the microbes within the body were relatively fewer. Mei Quanxi also made a pointed comparison: some flesh-body Buddhas in Fujian had their internal organs removed, while Jiuhua's did not — and internal organs, complex in tissue and rich in microbes, are normally the great enemy of preservation, so for Jiuhua's bodies to survive with their organs intact depended, in his view, all the more on thorough dehydration and sealing. He added one caution: the not eating or drinking before death has, in itself, no direct relation to preservation; what does the work is that once the stomach and intestines are emptied, the microbes inside the body diminish with them. Zhu Guosheng, speaking of the principle, does not commit himself either; the word he uses is "perhaps" — perhaps it has to do with air and moisture. His own experience agrees: by the time a vat is opened, the remains have shrunk drastically, somewhat like a mummy, and wrapping them in gauze and raw lacquer seals them into yet another, near-vacuum shell. He also noted that monks who were healthy in life achieve a flesh body more readily than those who were ill.

Set side by side, the two explanations do not actually collide head-on. One explains "why it did not decay," pointing to lime, charcoal, cool dryness, and sealing; the other explains "why him, of all people," pointing to decades of austere practice and the preparations at the end. The craftsman's trade is still being handed down, vats are still being sealed, and whether what comes out in three and a half years will have succeeded, no one dares guarantee in advance. Mount Jiuhua's mist refuses to lift for a third of the days in the year, and the golden bodies sit in their temples within that mist — the longest-sitting of them has sat there for more than twelve hundred years.
