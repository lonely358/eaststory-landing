Yang Zhen and the Mutton Soup: One Bowl of Soup That Lost a War

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~10 min read

In the second month of 607 BC, the armies of Song and Zheng drew up their formations at Daji. The chariot carrying Hua Yuan, commander of the Song army, suddenly moved — not toward its appointed position, but straight into the Zheng ranks, where it stopped. Hua Yuan, on the chariot, was a prisoner.

The man driving the chariot was Yang Zhen, Hua Yuan's own charioteer. Before he drove the chariot into the enemy formation, he left one sentence behind: "The other day, with the mutton, you gave the orders. Today, in this business, I give the orders."

The matter has to start with how this battle came about. Chu was strong at the time, and King Zhuang of Chu sent Zheng to attack Song. Prince Guisheng of Zheng led the troops pressing in, and on the Song side those who met them were Hua Yuan and Yue Lü. Hua Yuan was Song's Right Marshal, one of the ranking grandees of the court, and he held overall command of this battle. The two armies fought on the renzi day of the second month, at a place called Daji, in the area of present-day Zhecheng, Henan.

That year was the height of King Zhuang of Chu's bid for hegemony. Zheng had gone over to Chu only the year before. Song was an ally of Jin and sat across Chu's road north; Chu did not take the field itself, but sent Zheng to strike first. After this battle was lost, that summer Jin pulled Song, Wey, and Chen together to counterattack Zheng, evening the score for Song, so to speak. The real contest was between Jin and Chu; Daji was only one square on the board.

Before the battle, Hua Yuan did something commanders often do: he slaughtered sheep, cooked a mutton soup, and shared it among the officers and men to raise morale. When the sharing reached the end, his charioteer Yang Zhen had no portion.

The *Zuo Zhuan* gives this only five characters: "his charioteer Yang Zhen was not included" — it does not say why. The explanations later ages offered do not agree. Du Yu, commenting in the Jin dynasty, said there was not enough mutton soup to go around, and this one portion was short. Lü Zuqian of the Song dynasty guessed instead that Hua Yuan treated Yang Zhen as one of his own, so close that no ceremony was needed — and so he was overlooked. Which is true, the text does not say. The only thing certain is the result: Yang Zhen recorded the matter, and kept it recorded until the day of battle.

In his *Continued Discussions on the Zuo Zhuan*, Lü Zuqian gives another example that is almost identical. In the Warring States period, the Lord of Zhongshan entertained the scholars and officials of his capital, shared out mutton soup, and missed a man named Sima Ziqi. In his anger Sima Ziqi went to Chu, and when Chu later sent troops against Zhongshan, his instigation was behind it. Lü Zuqian's point was that people who form a mortal grudge over the smallest slight cannot be spotted in ordinary times; for Hua Yuan to keep such a man beside him, driving his chariot, was itself a failure of judgment.

In Spring and Autumn warfare, everything rode on chariots. Three men stood on a chariot; the driver held the middle, and the commander stood at the left. However great the commander's orders, where the chariot went and when it stopped were in the driver's hands, in the reins. Yang Zhen's place was ordinarily the lowest on the chariot; on the battlefield, what he gripped was the course of the entire army's commander. The commander's chariot was where the whole army's banners and drums were; hundreds of chariots and tens of thousands of foot soldiers watched that one chariot to advance or retreat. What Yang Zhen drove was not a chariot. It was the Song army's nerve center.

When the Zheng army pressed forward, Yang Zhen flicked the reins, and the chariot went into the Zheng formation. The Song army lost its commander, and its ranks fell into chaos. The losses the *Zuo Zhuan* records: Hua Yuan taken prisoner, Yue Lü killed, 460 war chariots captured, 250 prisoners taken, and 100 corpses with the left ear cut off. In his commentary Du Yu did the arithmetic for a chariot: one war chariot carried three armored men on the chariot and seventy-two foot soldiers below it. By that establishment, 460 chariots carted off by Zheng meant the Song army had lost about half its substance.

In the same battle there was another small man, named Kuang Jiao. He met a Zheng soldier in combat; the man, with no road left, jumped into a well, and Kuang Jiao turned his halberd around and reached down into the well to pull him out. The Zheng soldier climbed out of the well and, turning the tables, took Kuang Jiao prisoner. The *Zuo Zhuan*, through the mouth of "the gentleman," passed this comment: he lost both ritual propriety and his orders, and this end was not undeserved. The comment goes into detail: the rules of the army are daring, resolution, and obedience; to kill the enemy is daring, to be resolute to the end is firmness, and to do the reverse is to bring destruction on oneself. Kuang Jiao lost through softness of heart; Yang Zhen, through intent. One after the other, the two of them lost the Song army's face entirely. That day the commander was not the only unlucky man in Song, but no one's misfortune matched Hua Yuan's: the commander of a state's army, beaten by his own charioteer.

After the battle, Song offered a price to ransom its man. The price was one hundred war chariots and one hundred teams of fine horses. Fine horses are horses of handsome coat; one team is four horses, so one hundred teams made four hundred horses. A hundred war chariots, with men and equipment, was the cost of an entire army. Song was willing to pay it partly because Hua Yuan was a pillar of the state, and partly because the battle had hurt Song badly. When half the ransom had been delivered, the Zheng guards relaxed their vigilance, and Hua Yuan escaped on his own and ran back to Song.

A man who has escaped and returned still has rules to observe on entering the city. Arriving at the city gate, Hua Yuan did not go straight in; he stood outside the gate and first announced himself to the gatekeeper, and entered only when an answer came from inside. The first thing he did after entering the city was go to see Yang Zhen.

Yang Zhen has another name in the *Zuo Zhuan*: Shu Zang. Hua Yuan, seeing him, said a sentence that gave him every step down: "It was your horses, then." The meaning beneath the words: the chariot charging into the enemy ranks was not your fault; it was the horses.

Yang Zhen did not take the step down. He answered with five characters: "Not the horses. The man."

Having said this sentence, Yang Zhen left. Du Yu's note is very brief: when Shu Zang had finished speaking, he fled to Lu. He escaped to the state of Lu. The *Zuo Zhuan* writes nothing more of what became of him; the man's fate ends at those five characters, and the histories have no sequel.

Why Hua Yuan did not punish him on the spot was also debated by later ages. Lü Zuqian's explanation is that after a great defeat men's hearts were unsettled, and Hua Yuan's first use of "it was the horses" to steady Yang Zhen was a way of calming people; and moreover Song at that time knew only that its commander had been captured — it may not have known the chariot had been driven into the enemy formation deliberately. When Yang Zhen himself said "Not the horses — the man," it amounted to a confession to his face, and then flight was the only road left to him.

The battle was lost, but the city still had to be repaired. After Hua Yuan returned, Song strengthened its defenses against another coming of Zheng and Chu; he directed the wall-building and inspected the works in person. The conscript laborers building the walls made up a song and sang it at him to his face: "Goggle-eyed, big-bellied, / you threw away your armor and came back. / Unshaven, unshaven, / you threw away your armor and came back again." Hua Yuan sent a man to answer them: oxen still have hides, and the hides of rhinos and wild oxen are plentiful — what are a few suits of armor? The laborers topped it: there may be oxhide, but what about the red lacquer? Meaning, the materials are all there; it's for you to win back the face you lost. Hua Yuan waved his hand: "Let it go. Their mouths are many; mine is one. I can't out-argue them." A commander sung at to his face in a ditty by wall-building laborers, with the words written down in the histories character for character — that was not rare in those days. The rare thing is that Hua Yuan accepted the account and did nothing to those laborers.

A battle lost over a bowl of mutton soup ended, finally, in banter between the commander and a crowd of wall-building laborers. The *Zuo Zhuan*'s verdict on Yang Zhen is heavier than on anyone: "The gentleman said Yang Zhen was not human: for a private grudge he ruined his state and slaughtered its people — what punishment could be greater than this?" And it quotes the *Book of Songs*: what is called "a man without goodness" is a man like Yang Zhen.

Later generations gave this affair a name: "Yang Zhen's Grudge over the Soup" (*Yangzhen can geng*). The *can* here does not mean shame; in ancient Chinese the word could also mean resentment, a grudge nursed. Yang Zhen's *can*, from beginning to end, was hatred, not remorse.

One thing should be made clear: Song did not perish because of this battle. Hua Yuan returned home and governed as before, remaining Song's chief minister for many years. Thirteen years later, the Chu army besieged Song for more than half a year; in the city the grain ran out, to the point of exchanging children to eat them and splitting the bones of the dead for fuel, and still Song would not sign a covenant under the city walls. In the end Hua Yuan slipped into the Chu camp at midnight, climbed onto the bed of the Chu commander Zifan, woke him, and forced him to swear to withdraw thirty li, and only then did the two states make peace, with a covenant that read: "I will not deceive you; you shall not distrust me." A man who could do that was no muddle-headed official. When later ages tell the story as "a bowl of mutton soup destroyed the state of Song," that is exaggeration. The real price was 460 war chariots, several hundred lives, and a captured commander — heavy enough already. As for Yang Zhen, everything he left in the *Zuo Zhuan* is the name missing on the day the mutton was shared, the two sentences on the chariot, the five characters at the city gate, and one word: "fled."
