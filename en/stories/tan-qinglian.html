The Vanishing of Tan Qinglian: The Stock-Market Guru Who Packed the Hall, a Fugitive Unheard From for Twenty Years

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In 2006, Taiwan's Investigation Bureau, under the Ministry of Justice, published a wanted list of major economic fugitives. At the front of it stood Chen Yu-hao, chairman of the Tuntex Group, who had fled with enormous sums, and Chen Sheng-tien, chairman of Sampo Corporation. On the list there was one more name, one that younger investors no longer recognized: Tan Qinglian.

Yet only ten years earlier, this man could give a lecture in Taipei and pack the Social Education Hall until its doors nearly burst. That record has never been broken since.

Tan Qinglian was one of the most famous stock-market commentators of the "fourth channel" era. According to material compiled by his supporters, he started out in 1990 as a lecturer at a securities firm in Keelung and rode the hottest years of cable-television stock programs. There was no online streaming then; every day, retail investors sat in front of the television waiting for a teacher to call out the winning picks, and whoever was listened to by the most people could move the market for a day. And the number of people who listened to Tan Qinglian was beyond reason: when he called a buy, his members' money and the copycat money rushed in together; when he called a fall, not even a president's public appeals could hold the market up — at least, that is how his supporters tell it. What old investors remember most firmly is 1995: cross-strait tensions were high that year, missiles fell into the waters south of Taiwan, the wealthy were fleeing, and the market was in a panic sell-off. Just when everyone was cutting their losses and running, Tan Qinglian went on television every day shouting that you could sell anything, but you had to take the money and buy stocks, and have faith in Taiwan. Afterward the index climbed from 4,474 points all the way to 10,256, and those who had listened to him became the big winners of that wave.

Whether those days were as told, and how accurate his calls really were, his supporters and his critics argue to this day. But one thing is in the newspaper record: around 1996, his ten-thousand-person lectures jammed the doors of the Taipei Social Education Hall, and the funds moving in and out of the market at his word, by contemporary media accounts, could make up a substantial share of the whole market's turnover.

In the version passed down among his supporters there is an even more famous episode: in August 1997 the index peaked at 10,256 points; officials kept declaring that ten thousand points was the floor and 9,400 the line in the sand, but Tan Qinglian went the other way, publicly saying the index would first see 7,000, then be driven down to around 5,400. In the end the market really did fall, step by step, to 7,040, and then to 5,422. A construction stock he pushed hard, Yucheng Construction, soared against the trend and squeezed the short sellers into a chain of margin calls, until the regulators finally tailored trading restrictions to that single stock. Taiwan's stock market truly did gain a term from this — the "Yucheng clause" — denoting special trading rules set by the authorities for an individual stock. Where the term came from, every old investor knows it had to do with him.

In those years, the men who first stirred Taiwan's stock market were the money-backed "Four Heavenly Kings"; after them, the baton passed to Tan Qinglian's generation of advisory teachers. The *Liberty Times*, taking stock of that era later, said that after the Four Heavenly Kings, those who held sway over the market were the advisory teachers, and Tan Qinglian was a representative figure among them.

A 1998 CTS news report gives another set of numbers: from 1994 onward, Tan Qinglian set up investment associations and service offices one after another, taught his picks on fourth-channel programs under the name "Teacher Tan," drew in more than 30,000 members, and took in over NT$100 million in membership fees. In that report, the station's title for him was "God of Stock War." It was also in 1998 that he was sentenced to three years and six months for offenses including violations of the Securities and Exchange Act — the first fourth-channel stock guru ever sentenced.

Fame, money, and lawsuits came tangled together.

On the night of December 26, 2002, Tan Qinglian came out of a class on Kaifeng Street in Taipei and was forced into a taxi by four men, who held him for six hours. It was his first lecture at that venue; a poster for his class was still pasted on the security desk by the building's entrance. He was made to sign a promissory note for five million dollars and had a friend raise NT$900,000 in cash before he was released. The police assessment at the time was that the kidnappers had likely followed his picks, lost money investing, and seized him to demand payment. A CTS reporter afterward went and knocked on the door of his home; no one ever answered, and neighbors said it was actually his studio, with plenty of people coming and going in ordinary times. By then he had already been blacklisted by every television station for a while, and could only keep up appearances with the studio and his lectures.

In 2001 he had also appeared on a CTi TV program, blasting a big company's merger deal and a telecom company's accounts; the program was later pulled by the station in breach of contract. In his supporters' telling, this was the result of pressure from advertisers; what the station's side had to say, the public record does not follow up. Such episodes ran through his whole period of fame: every time he clashed with a big company or the regulators, it became a Rashomon affair — those who believed him believed him more, and those who hated him hated him more.

What truly made him vanish was a stock-manipulation case.

A Central News Agency report of March 2006 said that prosecutors had found that during 2004, Tan Qinglian, together with the financier Chen Hung-hsien and the market operator Hung Yung-yi and others, had used large numbers of nominee accounts to trade and rig the stock of Xiangsheng Electronics, driving its price from NT$9.85 up to NT$34.5, a rise of 250 percent. It began because Chen and Hung had lost money rigging another stock earlier and wanted Xiangsheng to fill the hole; short of funds, they brought in Tan Qinglian and others for financing and to find backers, the parties splitting the cost of the purchases in proportions of thirty and seventy percent. Beyond putting up money and making introductions, Tan Qinglian also touted the stock daily in voice commentaries on the "168 Finance Network" and the "Market Makers' Insider Fax Daily," which he edited, urging investors to rush in. The Taipei District Prosecutors Office indicted him together with three others on charges including violations of the Securities and Exchange Act and the Money Laundering Control Act.

And before the Xiangsheng indictment, he had already been convicted in another rigging case, involving Ruili. By the Central News Agency's account at the time, that case drew three years and two months; he refused to report for his sentence, his whereabouts were unknown, and he was already wanted. A later report by the *China Times* said instead that the High Court had given him a final sentence of three years and ten months, that in 2005, before serving any of it, he had fled the country and was hiding in mainland China, and that the Taipei prosecutors issued the warrant for him in January 2006.

The two reports' sentence figures do not match. But on one point they agree: the man ran.

The Investigation Bureau placed him on the list of major fugitive economic criminals, alongside Chen Yu-hao and Chen Sheng-tien. TVBS reported at the time that he was listed because his conviction was final and he had repeatedly failed to answer summonses. The financier indicted in the same case, Chen Hung-hsien — nicknamed "Shuixian," the Narcissus — was also a resounding name in the stock-market underworld of the day, and had earlier been listed for screening by the task force investigating the Liu Pang-you residence bloodbath. Set side by side, this batch of names amounts to roughly half the Taiwan stock-market underworld of that era.

When the *Liberty Times* looked back in 2014 at that generation of stock-market figures, its account of his ending took a single sentence: convicted of violating the Securities and Exchange Act, he fled to China before serving his sentence, and has been there ever since.

During his flight he had one more episode. In July 2005, Tan Qinglian, already abroad, gave a media interview alleging that in the Jingyong "vulture" case, the Financial Examination Bureau chief Lee Chin-cheng was closely connected to the market operator Lin Ming-ta, and claiming he had personally seen the two discussing short-selling stocks over a banquet table. Lee Chin-cheng held it was all fabrication and sued for defamation. The case dragged on until 2014, when the Taipei District Prosecutors Office issued a decision not to prosecute — not because what Tan said was true, but because he had been a fugitive for so many years that the statute of limitations had expired and the case could no longer be pursued.

That was probably his last "appearance" in the public record — and even that was across the sea, at second hand.

After that, there was nothing. For more than a decade since, no public report has said he was captured, surrendered, or returned to Taiwan. No one has photographed him, and no reporter has interviewed him again. Chen Yu-hao, on the same wanted list, held press conferences across the sea during his flight — quite the showman. Tan Qinglian was different: he never showed so much as a shadow again. The man who once, with a single word, could send ten thousand people surging into the Social Education Hall and set capital flowing in and out of the market to the sound of his voice has vanished completely from public view, for nearly twenty years now.

Where he is, what kind of life he leads, whether he still watches the market — no public report can answer. Of the crowd that once jammed the Social Education Hall's doors, some are still in the market and some left long ago; when someone occasionally brings up "Teacher Tan," all that remains is one sentence: nobody knows where he is now.
