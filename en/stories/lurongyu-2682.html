Thirty-Three Men Went to Sea, Eleven Came Back: The Bloody Voyage of the Lurongyu 2682

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~10 min read

On August 12, 2011, at Shidao Harbor in Rongcheng, Shandong, a distant-water squid-jigging boat was towed slowly into port by a fisheries enforcement vessel. On the day she sailed, the roster had carried thirty-three names. On this day, only eleven men were standing on her deck.

Police were waiting on the quay. The eleven were taken into custody the moment they stepped ashore. Their prepared story was that something had happened on board — that Baode and his men had killed people and then escaped on a wooden raft. The story did not hold for long. As the interrogations went deeper, the true shape of things emerged bit by bit: there had been no outside killers. The twenty-two men who never came back had all died at the hands of their own shipmates.

The boat was the Lurongyu 2682, owned by the Rongcheng Xinfa Aquatic Food Co., Ltd. Some forty meters long, she fished squid year-round in the waters off Peru and Chile in the southeastern Pacific. On December 27, 2010, she left Shidao with thirty-three crewmen. According to The Beijing News's reporting at the time, the contract promised two years at sea, pay of 45,000 yuan a year, plus a share of the catch; the work was jigging squid, boxing it, and freezing it.

Most of the thirty-three had been hired on casually; many were relatives or acquaintances. They came from Liaoning, Jilin, Inner Mongolia, Shandong, and elsewhere. Once the boat was at sea, the men naturally sorted themselves into three camps: a "Northeast gang" led by Liu Guiduo; an "Inner Mongolia gang" led by the Inner Mongolian Baode Gejirihu (the crew all called him Baode); and the captain and management, who came from Dalian. On a boat thousands of nautical miles from anywhere, these three camps were to eat and live together for two years.

The crack opened over money. After a few months, some of the crew realized the accounts did not add up. A crewman who later served his time and was released recalled to a reporter from *Esquire* (China) that they had been promised a guaranteed 45,000 yuan, but in fact they were paid by the weight of squid they caught — twenty-five fen (0.25 yuan) per jin — and only reached that figure if they caught enough. Liu Guiduo was quick with numbers. He did the math and said one sentence: "What we earn isn't even enough to buy cigarettes."

On June 16, 2011, the boat was in waters near Chile. Liu Guiduo went to have it out with the captain, Li Chengquan. According to the judgment, Li took a hard line and shot back that the official seal on the contract was fake — that this lot were illegal workers who had been smuggled aboard. That one sentence cut off every way out.

At about eleven o'clock that night, the seizure of the ship began. Liu Guiduo had Huang Jinbo and Wang Peng smash the communications equipment, posted Jiang Xiaolong and Liu Chengjian to guard the door of the wheelhouse and the ladder, and himself led men armed with the sharp knives used for dressing squid and with iron bars into the captain's cabin. Li Chengquan was beaten, stabbed, and tied with rope; his leg was slightly wounded. In the end he was forced to set a course home by satellite navigation, with Wang Peng at the helm. This was no spur-of-the-moment affair. According to the ruling, because they resented the long hours, the heavy work, and the low pay, Liu Guiduo had earlier drawn in Jiang Xiaolong, Liu Chengjian, Huang Jinbo, Wang Peng, and several of Baode's men, and plotted to seize the ship and sail her home. Who would smash the radios, who would hold the ladder, who would go into the captain's cabin — the jobs had all been assigned before they moved.

The first to die was the cook, Xia Qiyong. Hearing that something had happened to the captain, he charged up toward the wheelhouse with a knife in his hand, meaning to rescue him. At the ladder he was stabbed and beaten by Jiang Xiaolong, Liu Guiduo, and Liu Chengjian, and then thrown into the Pacific.

The ship had been seized, and no one ashore knew. The company found it could not reach the boat; when at last it got through to Li Chengquan, the captain, under the hijackers' coercion, said only one thing: the fishing boat is safe.

The ship turned for home. By the time she reached the waters west of Hawaii, the air on board had changed. Liu Guiduo began to suspect the chief engineer, Wen Dou, of secretly sabotaging the equipment and trying to rally a resistance. Informing and suspicion grow faster than anything else on a ship no one can leave.

On the night of July 20 into the 21st, the killing erupted all at once. That night Liu Guiduo directed operations from the wheelhouse and had music blasted over the loudspeakers to cover the sounds; Wang Peng and Mei Linsheng stood guard with knives in the bridge over Captain Li Chengquan and the chief mate. Wen Dou was first lured from the dormitory to the wheelhouse; as he came down the ladder, several men stabbed him and pushed him into the sea. His cousin Wen Mi was stabbed in his bunk in a four-man dormitory, asleep — Jiang Xiaolong and Liu Chengjian went in and did it. Liu Gang, Yue Peng, Jiang Shutao, and Second Mate Wang Yongbo were also killed one after another that night and thrown into the sea.

Next, the boatswain Chen Guojun and crewmen Wu Guozhi and Bo Fujun did not survive either. According to the Supreme People's Court's death-penalty review ruling, Liu Guiduo and the others demanded their bank cards and valuables; Huang Jinbo wrote down Bo Fujun's and Wu Guozhi's home addresses and telephone numbers, and then the men were stabbed down and kicked into the sea. In those same two days, the crewman Ma Yuchao disappeared; no one ever saw him again.

By now the ship was eleven men short. Liu Guiduo no longer dared go back to China — there was no explaining it. He changed his plan: they would slip into Japan as stowaways.

Stowing away took money. From July 23 to 24, he had the remaining crew call home on the satellite phone, inventing stories of illness and injury, and got their families to remit money to a bank card. The relatives of two of the victims, a man surnamed Shan and a man surnamed Qiu, believed the stories and sent 10,000 yuan in all. The money arrived; the men were not saved. On July 24, both were killed, one after the other.

That same day, Liu Guiduo heard a new piece of informing: Huang Jinbo said Baode resented the decision to go to Japan and was pulling men together for a mutiny. Liu Guiduo struck first again. Before moving, he found pretexts to take away the homemade knives held by a man surnamed Dai and a man surnamed Shuang, posted guards at the door of the twelve-man dormitory where the two slept, and then invented a story that they were going to kill Cui Yong, tricking Baode into handing his knife over to Li Chengquan. The moment the knife left his hand, Cui Yong, Li Chengquan, Huang Jinbo, and Wang Peng rushed him together; Baode was stabbed and forced to jump into the sea. The men of his camp — Shuangxi, Dai Fushun, Bao Baocheng, Shan Guoxi, and Qiu Ronghua — were also driven over the side. The man surnamed Dai jumped from the dormitory window into the sea; the man surnamed Shuang was never seen again.

In the small hours of July 25, a little after four, the ship herself got into trouble. The engine room was flooding, the ship lost power and began to list. Inspection found that the sea valve — the master valve to the sea — had been opened. Only two men knew where that valve was: Wen Dou, already dead, and the second engineer, Wang Yanlong. And Wang Yanlong was gone.

The ship was going down. Chief Mate Fu Yizhong, with Gong Xuejun, Ding Yumin, and Song Guochun, put on life jackets and escaped onto a homemade wooden raft loaded with supplies. The raft had not gone far before the current carried it back alongside the ship. Liu Guiduo and Li Chengquan had iron squid-jigging sinkers thrown at the raft, and Jiang Xiaolong leaped onto it and stabbed at the men with a fish spear. Fu Yizhong, Gong Xuejun, and Ding Yumin were forced into the sea and vanished.

Song Guochun cried for help in the water and was hauled back aboard. He did not stay alive long. Li Chengquan pointed out that Duan Zhifang and Xiang Lishan were the only two whose hands were not yet "bloodied." Liu Guiduo then coerced the two of them into tying Song Guochun with rope, fastening a hammer to him, and sinking him into the sea.

From that moment, every one of the eleven men alive on the ship had a killing on his hands. That was probably exactly what Liu Guiduo wanted — no one would come ashore clean, and so no one would inform on anyone else.

After saving herself, the ship had no way to move under her own power, and they had to switch the communications equipment back on and call for help. During the days of waiting for rescue, Liu Guiduo and Li Chengquan held meeting after meeting with everyone and fixed their pact of silence: they would say that Baode's gang had killed men and then fled on a raft. They bound the spare life jackets, the weapons, the victims' belongings, and a notebook in which the course of the case had been written down, weighted them with hammers, and sank them into the sea.

The distress call was first picked up, in waters east of Korea, by another Xinfa company fishing boat, and relayed up the chain. The Fisheries Command Center of the Ministry of Agriculture and the Maritime Search and Rescue Center of the Ministry of Transport in turn coordinated with the Japan Coast Guard and Chinese vessels nearby. On July 29, China Fisheries Law Enforcement vessel 118 arrived and towed the boat home. She docked on August 12, and the eleven were taken into custody. In their first statements, all eleven spoke with one voice: every man was innocent. Later, the statements were pried open one by one.

In 2016, *Esquire* (China) published a long reported feature, "A Survivor's Account of the Pacific Ocean Massacre"; the article about this boat was read more than thirty million times online. But no feature, however long, can tell the whole of those forty days — the only people left who can speak are the eleven who did the killing.

On November 14, 2012, the Weihai Intermediate People's Court opened the trial. On July 19, 2013, the first-instance verdict was announced: death for five men — Liu Guiduo, Jiang Xiaolong, Liu Chengjian, Huang Jinbo, and Captain Li Chengquan; death with a reprieve for Wang Peng; life imprisonment for Feng Xingyan; fifteen years each for Mei Linsheng and Cui Yong; five years for Xiang Lishan; four years for Duan Zhifang. On January 21, 2015, the Shandong Provincial High People's Court rejected the appeal and upheld the original verdict. On March 23, 2017, the Supreme People's Court approved the death sentences of the five principal offenders.

The final reckoning runs like this: thirty-three men went to sea; sixteen were killed; six went missing; eleven came back alive. Of the eleven who came back alive, not one was innocent.
