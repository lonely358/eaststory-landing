Di Qing: The Tattooed-Faced War God of the Song, Killed by a Rumour That His Dog Had Grown Horns

War & Upheaval · Nov 25, 2026 · ~11 min read

The Northern Song had a general with characters tattooed on his face, who wore a bronze mask into battle. In four years he fought twenty-five battles and took eight arrows. The emperor valued him and raised him step by step to Commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs — the highest post any general in the dynasty could hold. But when he finally died, it was not on a battlefield and not at court; it was at Chenzhou, where he had been banished, jumping at shadows all day, with a poisoned boil grown on his back, until he sickened and did not recover. What killed him was not an enemy. It was a rumour: that his family's dog had grown horns.

I. The Common Soldier with a Tattooed Face

Di Qing was born in 1008, a native of Fenyang in Shanxi, from a family that had farmed for generations. He was a fine horseman and archer, and as a young man went to the capital to enlist, and was assigned to the Gongsheng Army of the Palace Command. By the rule of the time, to keep soldiers from deserting, characters were tattooed on their faces. That one tattoo followed him all his life.

In 1038, Li Yuanhao of the Western Xia rebelled. Di Qing was picked for the frontier, starting as an attendant guard. He had a habit in battle: he would let his hair hang loose, put on a bronze mask, and charge at the very front every time. In four years he fought twenty-five battles, great and small, and was struck by arrows eight times. Jintang City, Youzhou, Anyuan — battle after battle, and the officers and men of the frontier all deferred to him. The administrative assistant Yin Zhu talked military strategy with him and found a man of real substance; Han Qi and Fan Zhongyan met him and said this was the makings of a fine general. Fan Zhongyan gave him a copy of the Zuo Tradition and said one sentence: "A general who knows nothing of past and present is merely a common brute." From then on Di Qing bent himself to study, and read through the arts of war of the generals and commanders of Qin and Han from beginning to end.

Afterwards he rose fast; in a little over ten years he climbed from a rank-and-file soldier to a high official of military-commissioner rank, and the tattoo was still on his face. Emperor Renzong wanted to bestow medicine on him to remove the characters; Di Qing pointed at his own face and said: "Your Majesty raised your servant for his merit and never asked about his birth. All that I have today, I owe to this tattoo. I wish to keep it, to encourage the men in the ranks, and I dare not obey the edict." The words spread, and Di Qing's name rang louder still through the army.

II. First Behead the Vanguard Commander, Then Take the Kunlun Pass

In 1052, Nong Zhigao of Guangyuanzhou rebelled, stormed Yongzhou, proclaimed himself the Renhui Emperor, changed the era name to Qili, and took nine prefectures in succession — Heng, Gui, Gong, Teng, Wu, Feng, Kang, and Duan among them. He besieged Guangzhou and failed to take it, then turned and attacked Hezhou, and killed a good many Song commanders. Several columns the court sent against him all ate defeats.

Renzong asked Pang Ji who could go south. Pang Ji said: "The Vice Commissioner of Military Affairs, Di Qing, once served under me. He is steady, brave, and full of stratagems; the southern affair can be entrusted to him." Di Qing also submitted a memorial volunteering for the task. In the ninth month he was appointed Commissioner of the Southern Court of the Palace Secretariat, Military Commissioner for Jinghu North Circuit, and Supervisor for the Suppression of Bandits in the Guangnan East and West Circuits.

When Di Qing reached the front, the first thing he did was not to march out, but to put the army in order. On the third day of the first month of 1053, he arrived at Binzhou, and Chen Shu, the Commissioner of Palace Services, who had fought against orders and failed, together with his thirty-one subordinate officers, was beheaded on Di Qing's order. The whole army was shaken; the histories say "the soldiers trembled in their legs, and all were bent on fighting with their lives."

On the night of the Lantern Festival, Di Qing divided his force into three columns, himself leading the vanguard in a forced march, and seized the Kunlun Pass in one move. On the eighteenth day of the first month, at the decisive battle of Guirenpu, the Song army attacked from front and rear, pursued for fifteen li (about 7.5 km), and took more than 2,200 heads and over 500 prisoners alive; fifty-seven of Nong Zhigao's generals and ministers were killed. Nong Zhigao fled to Dali. The next day the Song army entered Yongzhou, and Di Qing raised a jingguan — a mound of the enemy dead — to mark the victory.

When the dispatch reached the capital, Renzong said to his chancellors: "Settle the reward quickly; delay, and it will not suffice to encourage others!" In the fifth month, Di Qing was raised to Commissioner of the Bureau of Military Affairs, with the additional title of Acting Grand Guardian. A general risen from the ranks had reached the highest military office of the court.

III. Four Years as Commissioner of Military Affairs

Di Qing's becoming Commissioner of Military Affairs was the summit a Northern Song general could climb. But the Song exalted the civil and slighted the military, and for a general of humble birth to sit in that seat was in itself something that made the civil officials' bloc uncomfortable.

Worse still, he was too good a fighter, and too beloved of the army. The soldiers of the frontier deferred to him; the common people of the capital passed it around that he was the God of War star come down to earth. While Renzong was on the throne, he could still hold these discussions down. And the talk grew stranger as it spread: some said they had seen the Di family's dog grow horns, and strange lights rise over the house at night; and when the capital flooded in the Jiayou years, others said Di Qing had worn a yellow robe in the Daxiangguo Temple — and yellow was a colour only the emperor could wear.

Not one of these stories had any evidence behind it, and the histories themselves write plainly that they were false rumours. But at the court of that time, rumour itself was a charge. Di Qing understood it clearly too: he had risen from the ranks, with no family standing and no faction to lean on; the emperor's trust was his only support. The moment the emperor's suspicion stirred, he was nothing.

What a man who has climbed to the top by military merit fears most is never losing a battle; it is what comes after there are no more battles to fight, when others begin to reckon his account. In Di Qing's four years as Commissioner of Military Affairs, the fighting was finished, but the accounting grew finer and finer. The civil officials did not impeach him for rebellion; they impeached him for rumours — for the dog's horns, for the yellow robe — things that cannot be proven false, because there is no way even to begin to rebut them.

Renzong always treated Di Qing well. When Di Qing came back from pacifying Nong Zhigao, Renzong could not wait to settle his reward and raised him to Commissioner of Military Affairs. But an emperor's trust has its limit: Di Qing was a general, and the greater a general's merit, the more carefully an emperor must weigh him. The founding house rules of the Song stood there: the seat of Commissioner of Military Affairs a civil official might occupy, but a general might not occupy it too long. Di Qing knew in his own heart that the Commissioner's chair would not hold steady under him; what he did not expect was that before the chair had even warmed, the rumours would arrive first.

IV. Horns on the Dog, a Yellow Robe on His Back

In the eighth month of the first year of Jiayou (1056), Di Qing was dismissed from the capital, sent out as Military Commissioner of the Huguo Army with the honorary title of Joint Manager of Affairs with the Secretariat-Chancellery, to serve as prefect of Chenzhou. He had been Commissioner of Military Affairs for only four years.

At Chenzhou, Di Qing lived startled and suspicious all day long. The histories write that he was "in dread and doubt the whole day through"; a poisoned carbuncle grew on his back, and his illness deepened day by day. In the third month of the second year of Jiayou he died of illness at Chenzhou, at fifty years of age. Renzong mourned him, granted him the title of Director of the Chancellery, and gave him the posthumous name Wuxiang, "Martial and Accomplished." Two years later he was further granted the title of Director of the Department of State Affairs. After Emperor Shenzong succeeded, he had Di Qing's portrait brought into the palace and wrote a funerary address for him.

When Di Qing died, it was only four years since he had pacified Nong Zhigao. A man whom eight arrows on the battlefield could not kill died at the hands of a few rumours. The court gave him posthumous honours in full measure — but the man was gone, and honours are for the living to look at.

That he was frightened to death — the histories record only the symptoms and the circumstances, and draw no conclusion of cause and effect. I can say only this: he died of illness amid dread and suspicion. But one thing is certain: without that banishment, and without living every day inside other men's suspicion, a general just past fifty with an exceptionally strong constitution would not have collapsed so fast.

V. Why Him

Di Qing's death looks on the surface like the work of rumours; at the root it was the work of the system. At the Song's founding, Taizu took away his generals' commands over a cup of wine, and the rule he left behind was this: generals may fight battles, but they may not hold power, and still less may they hold prestige. Di Qing had all three: he could fight, his office reached Commissioner of Military Affairs, and soldiers and commoners alike deferred to him.

When Fan Zhongyan gave him the Zuo Tradition all those years ago, he meant him to grow from a common brute's courage into a commander who knew past and present. And Di Qing truly did it. But study could not change his birth, and military merit could not change suspicion. What the court truly feared was never whether Di Qing would rebel; it was that if he ever did rebel, no one could stop him.

Di Qing saw this clearly himself. When the emperor offered to remove the characters from his face, he refused, saying they were the reason he had what he had today. It was finely said, and sadly said: all his life he had been proving to the court — what I rely on is the tattoo on my face, is military merit, not any birth or pedigree. But in the end, it was exactly that merit and that fame that became his death warrant.

Epilogue: Whose Fault Is It When Merit Shakes the Sovereign?

After Di Qing's death, the folk made him into the God of War star descended to earth, assisting Renzong alongside Bao Zheng, the star of literature. That is of course legend, and cannot be taken as true. But the legend itself shows one thing: in the common people's hearts, Di Qing was a good general. The histories judge him well too: "He rose from the ranks, and his name stirred Chinese and foreigner alike; deep and steady, gifted with wisdom and strategy, he preserved himself from beginning to end through caution and awe." The pity is that he preserved himself to the end, but did not preserve his life.

If a dynasty cannot accommodate a general who has rendered great service, the problem does not lie with the general. Told to its end, Di Qing's story leaves behind, in truth, one old saying: when merit shakes the sovereign, it is never the general's fault — it is that the man seated above has taken fright. What Di Qing left to later generations is not only the legend of a war god, but a warning: if a system maintains itself by suspicion, in the end suspicion will turn and devour the very people it needs most.
