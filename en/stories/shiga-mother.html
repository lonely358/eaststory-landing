"I Defeated the Monster": After Nine Failed Years, She Killed Her Mother

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In March 2018, on the bank of the old Yasu River in Moriyama, Shiga Prefecture, Japan, the dismembered body of a woman was found.

The police investigated for three months, and on June 6 they arrested the dead woman's daughter. The daughter's name was Kiryū Nozomi, 31, the family's only child, who had just become a nurse at a university hospital. The dead woman was her mother, Kiryū Shinobu, 58.

Before the arrest, no neighbor had thought there was anything wrong with this family. Some recalled that mother and daughter had even gone out shopping together and seemed to get along well; a week before it happened, someone had seen the mother walking her dog and exchanging greetings. Other neighbors said the family kept to itself and had little to do with the people around them.

Only the daughter knew what life inside that house had really been.

When Nozomi was small, her father had moved into a company dormitory because of a job transfer. He worked in equipment maintenance, on duty day and night, and from her upper elementary school years onward, the home held only mother and daughter. From her early childhood, her mother had bought correspondence-course materials for her to study, with a single thought in her mind: her daughter would become a doctor. It was not that Nozomi had never had the dream herself — she loved Tezuka Osamu's manga Black Jack and had imagined becoming a surgeon. But through middle school and high school her grades simply would not rise, and by her third year of high school, what little spirit she had once had was worn away.

Neighbors later told reporters that the girl could study well enough, but was not much good at dealing with people. Her mother's whole mind was fixed on the examinations; in the daughter's world, besides her mother and the examination hall, there was almost no one else.

The mother would not let go. She required that her daughter apply only to medical schools at national universities within commuting distance. In 2005, Nozomi sat the entrance examination for the health sciences program of a national university's medical school, in her graduating year — and failed.

To her relatives, the mother lied and said her daughter had passed. And she made her daughter keep up the lie with her.

Court records and documentary interviews later disclosed things the mother had said in her lifetime, such as this: "If you don't become a doctor, you don't count as a person." That sentence was the law of that house. What the daughter was to make of her life had been decided long before; all that remained was to carry it out.

After the failure, the control tightened day by day. The mother confiscated her phone, left her not a moment of time to herself, and even bathed together with her, mother and daughter in the same tub, so that her daughter would never be out of her sight. Nozomi wanted to go out and work, to stand on her own; she was turned back — she was still a minor then, and everything she did required her mother's consent. She ran away from home three times. Every time, a detective hired by her mother, or police acting on a missing-person request, found her and brought her back to that house.

According to what was disclosed in the trial and in later interviews, when her grades would not rise, her mother made her kneel through the whole night apologizing, and had also laid hands on her. These details come from the court proceedings and from the defendant's own account; her mother is gone and cannot answer them. But one point the court did accept: the control in that house had long since gone beyond the bounds of a mother's ambition for her daughter.

Nine years passed in this way. Through nine examination seasons, year after year, she sat in the examination hall, and every application was her mother's choice.

In the ninth year, she finally passed: the nursing program at Shiga University of Medical Science. Not medicine — nursing. She thought she could breathe at last. After graduating, she became a nurse at the university hospital and started work.

Her mother's new order followed at once: what do you want to be a nurse for — become a midwife. Nozomi sat the selection for a midwifery course and was not chosen; that was the last attempt she ever made to step off her mother's track, and there was no next time. Her mother smashed her phone, called her a "traitor" and a "liar," and cursed her from daytime into the depths of the night, for a whole night. By Nozomi's account at the second trial, it was that night that broke her: she had been cursed countless times before, but this time — her phone smashed to pieces, kept awake all night under the abuse — something inside her snapped.

In January 2018, Nozomi began searching online: can a knife kill a person instantly; what happens if you cut the carotid artery. What she was looking up, originally, was a way to kill herself — by her later confession, in that period she had thought of dying, and had thought of killing her mother, and the two thoughts had tangled together. In the end, the one that came down was the second.

Late that night, her mother had her give a massage, as usual. Her mother lay face down, and as the massage went on she fell asleep, breathing evenly. Nozomi rose, took out the kitchen knife she had hidden in the bedroom, and stabbed down into the left side of her mother's neck. Her mother cried out, "It hurts," and in her fright Nozomi stabbed once or twice more.

Then she picked up her phone and posted a single sentence to her social media account: "I defeated the monster. Now I can rest easy."

After posting it, she turned on the television and watched the drama she had always wanted to watch and never had the chance to — BG: Shinpen Keigonin, about a civilian bodyguard. Her mother's body lay beside her, blood at the corner of its mouth. She finished the episode, covered the body with a blanket, and slept.

Over the month that followed, she cut her mother's body apart with a saw. The hands and feet went out mixed into the burnable garbage; the torso she put into a round bucket and discarded on the bank of the old Yasu River, only about 250 meters from home. By March 10, all of it was done.

In mid-March, the body was found, and the police spread their search along the riverbanks around Moriyama. Nozomi went to work at the university hospital as usual, as if nothing had happened. On June 6 she was arrested on suspicion of abandoning and damaging a corpse; in September she was arrested again, on suspicion of murder. She admitted the dismemberment and the disposal, but flatly denied the killing, saying her mother had committed suicide. Through the interrogations she held to that account, and in the courtroom she held to it still.

In February 2020, her trial opened at the Ōtsu District Court. She said the same thing: her mother had killed herself. But the court established that her mother had had no motive for suicide, and that on the night it happened there had been no one in the house but mother and daughter. In March, the verdict found her guilty of murder and sentenced her to fifteen years; the prosecution had asked for twenty.

The written judgment carried a passage to this effect: the defendant had grown up in a closed environment of only mother and daughter over many years, had remained subject to excessive interference even after reaching adulthood, and had been driven to a considerable extremity before committing the crime; there were circumstances that merited sympathy. At the end, the presiding judge said to her: you have always walked the track your mother laid down; from now on, walk your own life.

According to a journalist who interviewed her afterward, after the verdict Nozomi read the judgment over and over, feeling that "this person seems to have been standing beside us, watching us, all along." The years no one had understood had, for the first time, been written into a formal text. And it was after that, she told her lawyer: on appeal, I will admit it — I killed my mother.

The reason she had so stubbornly denied it at the first trial, she explained herself when the second trial opened: she was afraid of her father learning that his own daughter had taken a knife and stabbed her mother.

In January 2021, the Osaka High Court resentenced her: ten years. The second trial weighed the mother's years of educational abuse more heavily, finding that it was precisely the long years of physical and mental confinement and abuse that had driven the defendant to this point. The judgment became final in February 2021.

From fifteen years to ten — the five years taken off were the court's judgment on that house: the first trial had said her circumstances "merited sympathy"; the second said it more plainly — the root cause of the crime was the mother. As the verdict was read at the second trial, Nozomi now and then pressed her eyes with a handkerchief.

In Japan, this case is inseparable from one term: "educational abuse" — parents, in the name of education, subjecting a child to excessive interference, confinement, and oppression. Kyodo News, in reporting this case, said that it was this case that brought "educational abuse" onto the table as a social problem to be discussed.

From the first failure in 2005 to that late night in 2018, thirteen years. No third person ever entered that house; the mother's wishes were the whole of the law, and the daughter's life had only the one prescribed road. On the night the road reached its end, she posted that message — "I defeated the monster" — and watched an episode of a drama she had wanted to see for a long time.

After the case closed, the Kyodo News journalist Saitō Aya wrote her years of interviews into a book titled Haha to iu Jubaku, Musume to iu Rōgoku — which is to say: the mother a curse, the daughter a prison. The book records Nozomi's days in prison: she works on schedule, reflects earnestly, and accepts interviews and correspondence, telling the story of those years bit by bit. She once said a sentence: the days inside are easier to breathe through than the days she lived with her mother.
