The Dead Man Who Walked Home: Clairvius Narcisse's Eighteen Years

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~8 min read

One day in 1980, in the market of L'Estère, a small town in central Haiti, a man in rags walked up to a farm woman selling vegetables and spoke a childhood nickname that only family would know.

The woman's name was Angelina Narcisse. She stared at him. Eighteen years earlier, she had stood in the little cemetery north of the village and watched with her own eyes as her brother Clairvius's coffin was lowered into the pit and the earth shoveled over it, spadeful by spadeful.

The man said he remembered that day. He said he had been awake at the burial: he could hear his sister crying at his bedside, could hear the doctors pronounce him dead — he just could not move, and could not speak. As the earth fell on the coffin lid, he felt as if he were floating above the grave. On his right cheek there was a scar, which he said had been cut by a nail in the coffin.

He also said that on the night of the burial, a sorcerer had dug him out of the grave.

The Death Certificate from the Hospital

The hardest part of this story to get around is that Clairvius Narcisse's "death" exists in black and white.

On April 30, 1962, Narcisse walked into the Schweitzer Hospital in the town of Deschapelles. The hospital was run by American medical staff. He had a fever, ached all over, and had begun coughing blood. The doctors could not find the cause, and his condition worsened day by day. Three days later, around May 2, 1962, the doctors on duty — among them an American physician — pronounced him dead and signed the death certificate. The body lay in the cold room for some twenty hours, and then he was buried. By Narcisse's own account, he was conscious through all of it: he heard the doctors pronounce him dead, heard his sister crying, felt himself being placed in the coffin.

In Haiti, people claiming to have been zombies are not uncommon. Around 1980 alone, two other women appeared in other villages saying they were zombies, and farmers in the north reported finding a group of zombies wandering aimlessly in the fields. But Narcisse was different: the others had only stories; he had a death certificate issued by a proper hospital, and two doctors to attest to it. Those who later investigated his case called it the first "verifiable" zombie case.

The Eighteen Years, in His Own Telling

Narcisse's account went like this: on the night of the burial, a sorcerer — in Haitian Vodou, a practitioner of black magic is called a bokor — dug him up, fed him a kind of paste, beat him with a sisal whip, and took him to a sugarcane plantation in northern Haiti. There he worked the fields alongside others who had likewise been "brought back," like slaves, with no pay and no way to escape. Two years later, the bokor died, the drug they had been regularly fed was stopped, and he slowly came back to his senses and walked away from the plantation.

But he did not dare go home at once. He said the one who had done this to him was his own brother — the two had quarreled over land and inheritance, and he himself had drawn resentment in the village for abandoning his children. Only when he heard that his brother had died did he set out for the village. Sixteen years passed between his walking off the plantation and that day. Add the two years on the plantation, and from the burial to his walking into the market at L'Estère, it was exactly eighteen years.

The villagers were astonished at first, and then they believed him. Later, the psychiatrist Lamarque Douyon took up the investigation and, together with Narcisse's family, designed a set of questions — all private matters from the past that only someone raised alongside him could possibly know — and the man who called himself Narcisse answered every one correctly. Family, neighbors, and the doctors of that time all acknowledged that he was Clairvius Narcisse.

A Harvard Graduate Student Goes Looking for "Zombie Powder"

From 1961 onward, Douyon had been systematically collecting zombie reports in Haiti. He did not believe the dead came back to life, but he believed there was something real inside these stories: some drug that could slow a person's metabolism until it was almost undetectable, so that they looked dead; bury them, dig them up within a few hours, and then find a way to "wake" them. He put the idea to his American colleagues, and the New York psychopharmacologist Nathan Kline raised the money to send someone trained in ethnobotany to Haiti to find that drug.

The man sent was Wade Davis, a Canadian, then 28 years old, a doctoral student at Harvard under the ethnobotanist Richard Schultes. In 1982, Davis arrived in Haiti. He found Marcel Pierre, a bokor who ran a bar in the city of Saint-Marc, and paid him for powder, even watching him prepare it: toads, sea worms, lizards, tarantulas and other materials ground into a powder, one ingredient of which was human bone, taken by the two of them at midnight from the grave of a child they dug open in a cemetery. By Davis's record, the powder took effect when smeared on a victim's broken skin: within a few hours the person would begin to feel nauseous, have difficulty breathing, and go numb in the limbs; then came total paralysis, blue lips, and a metabolism sunk to a level almost impossible to measure — in as little as six hours, they were as if dead. Altogether he obtained five samples, each with a different recipe, but he concluded that the ingredients that truly did the work were two: tetrodotoxin, from the pufferfish, responsible for making a person "die"; and datura, responsible for keeping them, after being dug up, in a dazed and obedient state.

There was a real-world basis for this reasoning: cases of tetrodotoxin poisoning are extensively documented in Japan, where people who have eaten pufferfish can remain conscious yet unable to move or speak, and some have been pronounced dead and then come back to life. Davis matched this thread to Narcisse's account of lying awake through his own funeral, and concluded the mystery was solved. In 1985 he published *The Serpent and the Rainbow*; the book sold widely and in 1988 was adapted into a horror film of the same name. Narcisse's story spread around the world from there.

Davis himself, though, left a tail on the story in his book: he did not believe the drug alone could make a zombie. Japanese pufferfish-poisoning victims wake up as patients; they do not believe they are dead men. The reason Haiti's victims submitted, he argued, was that they and everyone around them believed zombies were real, and the drug was only the first push into that belief. This argument was often quoted afterward, and just as often turned back on him by critics: if cultural pressure is the key, they asked, then whether the powder actually contained the toxin demanded hard evidence all the more.

Then the Laboratory Said Something Else

The challenges arrived almost as soon as the book became famous, and they came from the world of toxicology.

In 1986, the toxicologists C. Y. Kao and Toshio Yasumoto published a paper in the journal *Toxicon* whose conclusion was blunt: the widely circulated claim that "tetrodotoxin is the culprit behind zombification" had "no factual basis." They and other laboratories analyzed the powder samples Davis had brought back, testing them repeatedly, and found no tetrodotoxin in these preparations at levels sufficient to take effect; in some samples, none could be detected at all. In a 1988 interview, Kao put it more directly still: he believed the affair involved "scientific fraud." Bo Holmstedt of Sweden's Karolinska Institute chose slightly softer words, saying Davis had not deliberately committed fraud but accusing him of concealing data that did not support his conclusions, and calling the work "bad science."

Davis did respond formally: he said recipes varied greatly from place to place, that the way the toxin dissolved might affect the test results, that other ingredients in the recipe might help the toxin cross the blood–brain barrier and act at extremely small doses; and he said that such a thing could never succeed every time in the first place — one success was enough to sustain the whole zombie belief. The critics were not persuaded. By 1990, the mainstream of the field no longer accepted the tetrodotoxin hypothesis; after the early 1990s, discussion of this line of inquiry essentially vanished from serious academic literature, and only popular books kept telling it.

So the parts of this affair that can be settled today have to be looked at separately. The man Narcisse, the 1962 death certificate, the family's recognition of him in 1980 — these are on record and can be checked. Whether he was really drugged, dug out of his grave, and worked two years on a plantation rests on his own telling alone, with no corroboration. Whether that powder contained anything that could "lay a man out" — the laboratory's answer was no, at least for the samples Davis provided. The rest — where exactly he spent those eighteen years, for instance — no one can fill in.

In 1994, Clairvius Narcisse died and was buried at L'Estère. This time, he did not come back.
