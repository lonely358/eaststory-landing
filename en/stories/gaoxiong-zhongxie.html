The Daughter the Third Prince Called Home: The Wu Family of Six in Kaohsiung, and One Month

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In April 2005, police walked into a townhouse on Yumin Street in the Gushan District of Kaohsiung. Every window in the house was pasted over with paper talismans, and a whole row of black clothes hung drying outside. The family's eldest daughter had just been pronounced dead. The cause was not ghosts or gods; it was days without food, and multiple organ failure.

The family was surnamed Wu. The father, Wu Wuyun, and the mother, Wu Cai Yueqing, made their living from odd jobs. All four children were in their twenties: the eldest daughter, Wu Jinnu, worked in Taipei — according to later retrospective reports, she and her boyfriend ran a boxed-lunch shop there; the son, Wu Wangyuan, worked at a printing plant; a daughter, Wu Meihong, was a nurse; and the youngest daughter, Wu Meiyi, was the baby of the family. More than the six of them lived in the townhouse; relatives lived downstairs. The family were devout believers in folk religion, and they kept an altar to the Third Prince at home.

It began with the youngest daughter. In February 2005, Wu Meiyi started feeling unwell, as if something unclean had fastened onto her. According to the retrospective reports, the family took her to a folk temple in Nanzi for a soul-recalling ritual. That night, back home, she suddenly "went into trance" — what folk belief calls possession by a god. She declared herself possessed by the Third Prince, and through her mouth he told the whole family: the eldest sister, Wu Jinnu, in Taipei was in mortal danger; she had to come home to Kaohsiung before the end of February, or her life would be forfeit.

The Wu family believed it. The mother traveled to Taipei overnight and brought Wu Jinnu back to Kaohsiung.

After coming home, Wu Jinnu could not sleep well. She said that the moment she fell asleep she had nightmares of being violated, and from then on she only dared to sleep in the daytime. In early March she took a phone call. According to what the family later said, it was after that call that Wu Jinnu also went into trance, declaring herself possessed by Guanyin, the Goddess of Mercy, and she began to hit herself and harm herself.

The family panicked. They took her to meditate at Wuzhishan and to a spirit altar in Nanzi District for another soul-recalling. Nothing improved. Back home, the thing spread instead: one after another, everyone in the house was "possessed." The father declared himself the Jade Emperor; the mother, the Queen Mother; a daughter, the Seventh Fairy. The gods had gathered a full table — but daily life was over.

Over roughly the next month, what happened in that house was later written into police statements: the six of them beat one another with ancestral spirit tablets and canes, and burned one another's bodies with lit incense sticks. They believed that scattering salt and rice could drive out evil, and that forcing excrement into someone's mouth and smearing urine on their body could exorcise demons. For many days they ate no proper food, drinking only talisman water, and said they felt no hunger. In the younger siblings' thinking at the time, striking their eldest sister with objects was not hitting a person; it was helping her drive out a ghost.

The whole family stopped going to work. The relatives downstairs were too frightened to come home and moved into a hotel. Neighbors later told reporters that during that period the family acted possessed and strange, hardly ever came out, and that sounds of fighting — bang, clang — kept coming from the house, mixed with screaming; it was hair-raising, and no one nearby dared go near the Wu home. Neighbors also saw members of the family suddenly go into trance right outside their own door, raving incoherently. What was happening inside the door, outsiders could only hear.

On April 4, the family took Wu Jinnu to an aunt's home for a soul-recalling; passing a house in Nanzi District, Wu Jinnu spat at its occupants with malice. They called the police. Officers arrived, took her for a mental patient, and sent her back to the home on Yumin Street. The door closed behind her, and stayed closed for five more days.

On April 9, Wu Jinnu's breath stopped. According to the *China Times*'s later retrospective, that night the family found her foaming at the mouth, her body stiff and cold, her heartbeat already very faint. Her two younger sisters performed CPR on her for a few minutes, and then stopped. No one called an ambulance, and no one took her to a hospital. In the whole family's understanding at that moment, what lay there was not Wu Jinnu; what had died was only the demon attached to her.

The body simply stayed in the house. Two days later, the family finally had a neighbor report it, and Wu Jinnu was taken to Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital. She could not be saved.

The forensic autopsy's conclusion was calm: Wu Jinnu had died of multiple organ failure caused by many days without food, and her body bore marks of burns from incense. According to the retrospective reports, doctors and the coroner noted on examination that most of the burns on her body were round, as if made one press at a time by the lit tip of an incense stick. She was not carried off by a ghost. She starved to death, in her own home, beside her parents and her younger siblings.

When the case was investigated, the Wu family was gone. According to a retrospective compiled later by Tiantian Kankan, they had hidden at relatives' homes, saying they feared the evil spirits in the house would come for them again. Police called the number on the household registration; the man who answered was Wu Wuyun himself. He said he was willing to cooperate with the investigation, but the police first had to agree to one condition: bring from a temple a ritual implement that had been consecrated by an enlightened monk. The police did as asked. On the day the implement arrived, the Wu family appeared at the police station together. Hearing the news of Wu Jinnu's death, the whole family was unnaturally calm and spoke with one voice: Wu Jinnu had not died; what died was only the evil spirit on her. Then one day the mother claimed to be possessed by Wu Jinnu, and through the mother's mouth Wu Jinnu "personally" announced her own death. Only then did the family accept that their daughter was truly dead.

Prosecutors and police advised the family to undergo psychiatric evaluation. The result surprised many people: the five surviving members had no mental illness at all.

In an interview, Tseng Tung-sheng, director of psychiatry at Kaohsiung Armed Forces General Hospital, gave his judgment: the family looked more like a case of shared, familial collective delusion. One person in the household falls into delusion first, and the others, in the closeness of living together, reinforce it in one another. He said that people with psychotic delusions are the group least likely to seek medical care in psychiatry, because the patients do not believe they are ill at all; and even if there are mild symptoms ordinarily, as long as work and social dealings are unaffected, neighbors and colleagues cannot tell. He also noted that one member of this family was herself a medical worker, with medical training, and still firmly believed the family was not ill but possessed by ghosts — which showed how deeply some of these notions were rooted in this household.

Others raised doubts from within religion itself. Ke Tianshuang, chairman of the Kaohsiung Taoist Association, said publicly that the Third Prince is a "righteous god" who could not possess someone at random, and that he doubted the Wu family's claim to have been possessed by him; he also said that all religions counsel people toward good, and none would watch someone die without helping.

As for the folk metaphysics circle, its version was different again. In 2021, when the television program *Tu Fa Qi Xiang* revisited the case, a media figure analyzed it on the show from a metaphysical angle, saying the Wu family's house was suspected of sitting in a "road-rush" position with an irregular plot — a legendary haunted house — and that the family had drawn in evil spirits while moving the statue of the Third Prince in their home. These claims were told on the program in vivid detail, but they and the forensic autopsy conclusion were never on the same page.

Wu Wuyun told prosecutors that at the time the whole family was in a daze; he knew something was wrong with Wu Jinnu, but he felt that it was no longer his daughter, and so he did not get her to a doctor in time. According to the *China Times*'s reporting, the family's account was this: everyone was in trance, their minds unclear, and when they came to their senses they found the eldest daughter collapsed and near death, and rushed her to medical care; they admitted no wrongdoing. But the same *China Times* report also wrote that on the night of April 9 Wu Jinnu was already failing, that the family performed only a few minutes of first aid, and that two days passed before they had a neighbor report it and send her to hospital. Whether the two accounts square with each other, the reporting is there for readers to see for themselves.

The prosecutors did not accept the Wu family's account. They determined that the five were in normal mental states, yet had watched Wu Jinnu waste away from not eating and had not rescued her, and indicted all five for abandonment causing death.

In April 2007, a *Liberty Times* reporter went to find the Wu family again. Wu Wuyun said he was innocent and refused the interview.

Later still, neighbors pooled money to hire a ritual master to exorcise the house and perform rites for the dead, and the Wu family slowly returned to everyday life — except that they no longer wished to speak of that month.

The family was remembered by the public once more. In 2022 the Taiwanese film *Incantation* was released to hot box office and hotter discussion; the film was adapted from this very case. The village, the rituals, and the curses in the film are fictional embellishments, but the story's foundation is that house on Yumin Street, pasted over with talismans.

On Wu Jinnu's death certificate there is no "Third Prince," and no "Guanyin." What is written there is organ failure.
