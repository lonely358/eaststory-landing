Lady Xin Zhui: A Body Undecayed for Two Thousand Years, with 138 Melon Seeds Still in Her Stomach

Strange Cases · Nov 21, 2026 · ~10 min read

In the winter of 1972, a woman's corpse lay on the dissecting table of Hunan Medical College. She had been buried underground for 2,100 years, yet her skin still had its elasticity, her joints could still move, and her eyelashes and fingerprints were perfectly clear. The surgeon in charge opened her stomach and intestines and counted out 138 undigested sweet melon seeds. Two or three hours before she died, she had still been eating melon.

The woman was named Xin Zhui, wife of Li Cang, Chancellor of the State of Changsha in the Western Han. Her tomb is at Mawangdui in Changsha. In the winter of 1971, the PLA's 366 Hospital was digging an air-raid shelter and struck blue ghost-fire — and so she was brought up out of the ground.

I. Ghost-Fire Rising from an Air-Raid Shelter

In December 1971, the 366 Hospital was digging an air-raid shelter beside Mawangdui. As they dug, blue flames began to leap from the hole; the workers were terrified and downed tools, refusing to go on. The construction team hurriedly reported to the Hunan Provincial Museum, which sent Xiong Chuanxin with a team to look. One inspection settled it: the shelter had dug into a Han tomb.

That Mawangdui might have Han tombs beneath it had long been suspected. In 1952 the archaeologist Xia Nai came to look and judged that Han tombs lay beneath the two earthen mounds, but they were never excavated. Halfway through digging an air-raid shelter, a two-thousand-year-old Han tomb collided with the present of its own accord.

The name Mawangdui itself has a history. The two mounds stand side by side in the shape of a horse's saddle, and were originally called Ma'andui, "Horse Saddle Mounds"; the local accent slurred it into Mawangdui. In 1972 the 366 Hospital's address plate still read "No. 2, Ma'andui." In the early Northern Song, the place was called the Tomb of the Two Women, and was for a time believed to be the tomb of Tang Ji and Cheng Ji, the two mothers of Liu Fa, Prince of Changsha of the Han. Another account holds that the name comes from a decoy tomb of Ma Yin, King of Chu in the Five Dynasties period. The name changed several times over; who lay beneath, no one knew.

II. Six Thousand Yuan Dug Up a National Treasure

The Hunan Provincial Museum decided to excavate. It applied for 12,000 yuan of funding and was granted 6,000; there was not even enough for tools, and nearly everyone in the museum went up onto the site. The excavation happened to fall during the Cultural Revolution; most of the museum's staff had been sent down to the countryside for labour, and only some thirty people could be counted on to work each day — among them several old masters who had once been tomb robbers, and it was on them that the team relied to find the tomb's extent.

The formal excavation began on January 16, 1972. First the cement base of a pavilion on top of the sealing mound was demolished; in four days they reached the white sealing clay above the tomb mouth. Beneath the white clay lay a layer of charcoal, and beneath the charcoal, the coffin chamber. Outside the chamber were packed white sealing clay and more than ten thousand jin of charcoal (a jin is about 0.5 kg, so over five metric tons), and the coffin itself was nested as three outer coffins and three inner coffins, sealed layer within layer.

The excavation took sixty days in all and was essentially finished by April 28. More than a thousand artifacts came out: the plain gauze robe, the T-shaped silk painting, 184 pieces of lacquerware, 312 bamboo slips — and one seal, carved with the three characters "Qie Xin Zhui" ("the wife Xin Zhui"), which is how her name is known. The coffins fitted into one another with exact tightness; between the outer and middle coffins were four side compartments holding grave goods; coffin within coffin, without a seam.

At one point the site fell into chaos. When word spread that the coffin chamber had been found, the crowds of onlookers grew and grew; at the peak, more than a hundred police were keeping order on site and barbed wire had been strung around it. The archaeological team had to shift the main excavation work to the night. Three early robbers' tunnels were also found during the excavation, but none of the three had pierced the white sealing clay; the tomb had never been robbed, and not a single thing was missing.

The T-shaped silk painting from Tomb One is 205 centimetres long and depicts a soul being led up to heaven; it remains a treasure of the Hunan Provincial Museum to this day. The silk manuscripts unearthed from Tomb Three include the Laozi, the Yijing, and the Zhanguo Ce, along with two maps charting the river systems of what is now southern Hunan. The texts on the silk differ in a great many places from the versions handed down today, and historians are still collating them against the received texts.

When the body first came out, it was put on display in a Changsha park without any treatment at all; the crowds were enormous and rumours of every kind flew. Yao Wenyuan of the Central Cultural Revolution Group demanded that the body be cremated; the museum refused, on the ground that no official directive had come down. Zhou Enlai telephoned his instructions directly: the body was transferred overnight to Hunan Medical College, disinfected and preserved against decay, and properly kept. Only then did the later autopsy take place.

III. Who She Was

The tomb's occupant was Xin Zhui, wife of Li Cang, Chancellor of the State of Changsha. Li Cang was the first Marquis of Dai, enfeoffed by Liu Bang, Emperor Gaozu of Han. The name Xin Zhui comes from a seal unearthed in her tomb, inscribed in intaglio seal script "Qie Xin Zhui" — though some say the last character should be read "Bi," and whether she was Xin Zhui or Xin Bi remains a matter of scholarly dispute.

Her husband Li Cang lies buried in Tomb Two beside hers; three seals were found in the tomb, and a jade personal seal carved with the characters "Li Cang" corrected an error in the records of the Shiji and the Hanshu. Her son is buried in Tomb Three; a wooden tablet shows he was buried in 168 BC. Three tombs, a family of three, under the same earthen mound.

Xin Zhui died at about fifty years of age, in the early Western Han, more than 2,100 years ago. In life she was the Chancellor's wife — beneath one man and above ten thousand in the State of Changsha — and her food, clothing, and daily goods were the finest of the age. The things unearthed from the tomb are exactly what her days looked like: she ate from lacquerware, wore silk, listened to the se and the yu, and was attended by 162 wooden figurines. The bamboo-slip inventory of the grave goods reads like the account book of a household's possessions.

When she died, the future Emperor Wu of Han would not be born for several decades. In the early Western Han, the world had just recovered from the wars at the end of Qin; Changsha was one of the largest feudal states of the south, and the days the Chancellor's wife lived were among the finest days under heaven at that time.

IV. The 138 Melon Seeds in Her Stomach

The autopsy was performed in the winter of 1972, by the surgeon Peng Longxiang. He opened Xin Zhui's stomach and intestines and counted out 138 undigested sweet melon seeds. Together with the pathological report, the doctors inferred that she died within two or three hours of eating the melon, and that the cause of death was very likely a sudden heart attack.

Her body told the doctors more: she suffered from gallstones, coronary heart disease, and schistosomiasis japonica. Today, coronary disease together with gallstones, and a sudden heart attack at fifty, would be nothing strange at all. What is strange is that her body, buried underground for 2,100 years, could still let doctors make such a diagnosis. Schistosomiasis and coronary arteriosclerosis are not fatal today, but two thousand years ago, when a fifty-year-old woman's heart suddenly failed, there was no one at her side who could save her.

Those 138 melon seeds were the last meal she left behind. Summer, the sweet melons had just come in; she ate a good many of them, and before they were digested, she was gone. Two thousand years later, people opened her stomach and saw nothing but a meal left unfinished.

V. Why She Did Not Decay for Two Thousand Years

Xin Zhui's remains are the longest-preserved wet corpse in the world. That they could survive 2,100 years comes down to the tomb itself being sealed tight — that is the true reason the body did not decay.

Her tomb pit was nearly 20 metres deep; the sealing mound rose more than 20 metres; the tomb mouth was 19.5 metres long and 17.8 metres wide. Outside the coffin chamber they packed first white sealing clay, then more than ten thousand jin of charcoal; the coffin was nested in three outer and three inner coffins, layer within layer, and the whole chamber was sealed fast. The white sealing clay is a mixture of lime and clay with excellent sealing properties; the charcoal absorbs moisture; the coffins fitted with exact tightness. Deep burial, sealing, absence of oxygen, sterility — with all four together, bacteria could not get in, and the body could not rot.

Whether the liquid inside the coffin had a preservative effect is still debated among scholars. Some say the liquid was a preservative poured in beforehand; others say it formed of itself from the body in the sealed environment. Which it was, there is no settled answer to this day.

After the autopsy, the body showed signs of decalcification and was only saved by emergency conservation. Today Xin Zhui's remains are kept at the Hunan Provincial Museum, at constant temperature and humidity, and are not lightly shown. In 2005, Central South University and the Hunan Provincial Museum jointly founded a conservation research centre devoted to studying how she can be preserved further. Not decaying for two thousand years was hard enough; preserving her for another two thousand will be no easier.

Epilogue: Her Last Meal Has Been Eaten for Two Thousand Years

What moves people most in Xin Zhui's story is not how many treasures were in the tomb, but those 138 melon seeds. A chancellor's wife, still eating melon two hours before she died, and enjoying it — never knowing it was her last meal.

What archaeology digs up is never only artifacts; it is the shape of lives once lived. Light as the plain gauze robe is, exquisite as the T-shaped silk painting is, neither is as solid and real as the 138 melon seeds in her stomach. They are the seeds a fifty-year-old woman left uneaten on a summer afternoon two thousand years ago. The melon she ate, the clothes she wore, the lacquerware she used are all in the Hunan Provincial Museum today, and the visitors queue to see them. She did not know she would die; still less did she know that two thousand years after her death, people would count her out, seed by seed.
