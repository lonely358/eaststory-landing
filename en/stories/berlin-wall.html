The Fall of the Berlin Wall: One Sentence Said Wrong, and the Wall Fell That Night

World Curiosities · Nov 20, 2026 · ~10 min read

On the evening of November 9, 1989, at six o'clock, Günter Schabowski, spokesman for East Germany, walked into a press conference with a note in his pocket — a note he had received only just before the meeting and had never properly read. A little over fifty minutes later, an Italian journalist pressed him on when the new travel regulations would take effect. Schabowski glanced through the note and answered: "To my knowledge, effective immediately." The plan had been for the rules to take effect the next day, and with visas required. Once the words were out, West German television broadcast them that very night, and tens of thousands of East Berliners poured toward the checkpoints. Before midnight, the barriers at the Berlin Wall's checkpoints had been raised. A wall that had stood for twenty-eight years fell, just like that, in a single night.

I. One Wall, Twenty-Eight Years

The Berlin Wall went up overnight in August 1961, cutting one city into two halves. The wall ran 155 kilometers in all (about 96 miles) and stood 4 meters high (some 13 feet); between its two concrete walls lay a "death strip" sown with mines, and in 1989 there were still 302 watchtowers. Over twenty-eight years, hundreds of people were shot dead by border guards while trying to climb it. The wall's official name was the "Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart," and the people on the other side of it all understood: what it protected against was not an attack coming from the West, but people escaping from the East.

In the summer of 1989, the wall began to loosen from the outside. In May, Hungary removed the barbed wire along its border with Austria; in August, Hungary opened the border. Thousands upon thousands of East Germans, under cover of holiday trips to Hungary, crossed the border into West Germany and never came back. At the end of September, several thousand people crowded into the West German embassy in Prague, demanding to be let out. On October 18, Erich Honecker, who had ruled East Germany for eighteen years, stepped down, and Egon Krenz succeeded him. On November 4, half a million people rallied on Alexanderplatz in East Berlin to demand reform. On November 6, the Interior Ministry produced a draft of new travel regulations, and the public cursed it as "complete garbage." On November 7, the entire government resigned.

The Krenz government could hold out no longer and decided to loosen things a little: citizens would be allowed to apply to leave the country — a way of letting off some steam. The new regulations were drawn up, set to take effect on November 10; citizens would first have to go to a police station and apply for a visa, and the press release was prepared to go out on November 10 as well. No one expected that on the evening of November 9, one day early, the regulations would be read aloud.

II. How the Note Came About

On November 9, the East German Politburo met and settled the new travel regulations. Schabowski was a member of the Politburo and the government spokesman, but he had not taken part in the discussions that drafted them, and he was not clear on the details of the new rules. Shortly before the press conference began, Krenz handed him a note bearing the main points of the new regulations. No one told him how the thing was to be presented, or whether it could be announced at once.

At six in the evening, the press conference began, broadcast live on East German television and radio. Schabowski sat at the table, with the minister of foreign trade and several members of the Central Committee beside him. The conference ran for an hour, and the early part was all routine questions. At about 6:53, Riccardo Ehrman, the Italian correspondent for ANSA, asked whether the travel law draft of November 6 — the one cursed as garbage — had been a mistake. Schabowski first wandered a little, saying that West Germany no longer had the capacity to take in any more refugees; then he suddenly remembered the note in his pocket, and said that a new regulation had been drafted, allowing citizens to leave the country permanently through all border crossing points.

There was a stir in the room. None of the journalists had seen these regulations, and they pressed him with questions from all sides. Schabowski began to read from the note. A journalist in the front rows asked: when do these regulations take effect? He hesitated for a few seconds, then answered: "To my knowledge, effective immediately, without delay." The German original runs: "Das tritt nach meiner Kenntnis ... ist das sofort ... unverzüglich."

He had answered wrong. The second point on the note did indeed read "effective immediately" — but that was the wording of an internal transitional provision. The regulations as a whole were meant to take effect the next day, and the press release was scheduled for November 10. Schabowski had read an internal document that had not yet reached its moment of publication aloud to the whole world. Another journalist pressed on: do these regulations include the crossing points to West Berlin? He turned to the third point on the note and read it out: they do. At seven o'clock sharp, the press conference ended.

III. After Television Carried It

The press conference had not yet broken up when the news was already out. At 7:04 p.m., West Germany's dpa news agency sent a flash: East German citizens could cross the border "immediately." At 7:17, Heute, the news program on ZDF, the second West German channel, broadcast footage from the press conference. At 8:00, Tagesschau on the first channel put the story at the top of the news. East Berliners sitting at home, watching West German television, heard a single sentence: the wall is open, and you can go right now.

People began to walk toward the checkpoints. First a few dozen, then a few hundred; by ten o'clock, more than ten thousand had gathered outside the Bornholmer Straße checkpoint. The border guards were given orders: check passports first, and for those who made the most fuss, stamp the passport — which amounted to stripping them of their citizenship and barring them from ever coming back. But the crowd kept growing, and the stamping could not keep up. The officer on duty telephoned his superiors for instructions; at the other end of the line, no one dared to make the decision: no one was willing to give the order to shoot, and no one dared to give the order to open the gates.

Before midnight, Harald Jäger, the commander of the Bornholmer Straße checkpoint, made a decision. He had received no order from any superior; he gave the order himself: raise the barriers, let the people through. At about 11:30 p.m., the barriers went up, and the crowd poured toward West Berlin. On the West Berlin side, people had long been waiting in the streets to greet them, with champagne and flowers in their hands. The other checkpoints soon did the same. That night, the Berlin Wall became a piece of stage scenery.

IV. The Wall Was Not Pushed Down

Many people imagine that the Berlin Wall was pushed down by the crowds that night. It was not. No one pushed the wall down that night; the border guards opened the gates themselves. The actual demolition of the wall was the work of the months that followed. The Brandenburg Gate was not opened to pedestrians until December 22. The wall was taken apart piece by piece; some pieces were sold as souvenirs, and some went into museums.

Jäger later recalled that what he thought that night was very simple: if the gates did not open, the crowd would storm the checkpoint, and then people would certainly die. He did not want his own men to open fire, and he did not want to watch people crushed and trampled to death either. Some of his superiors afterward said he had disobeyed orders; others said he had saved everyone. Jäger did not argue the point much. He said that anyone sitting in that position that night would have made the same decision. Whether that is true, no one can verify — but that night, it is a fact that no one gave the order to shoot, and a fact that no one died in front of the checkpoint.

Schabowski's sentence has been argued over ever since: did he actually say it wrong? Some research points out that the second point on his note really did say "effective immediately," and that he merely read out what was written. The point in dispute is that he treated an internal transitional provision as a decision that could be publicly announced. But on one thing all sides agree: the East German regime never intended to open the wall that night. What the Krenz government wanted was to let off a little steam and buy some time; it never imagined that once the steam was released, the whole lid of the pot would fly off.

V. What Actually Happened That Night

Lay the day of November 9 out flat, and the timeline is exact to the minute: in the afternoon, the Politburo met and settled the new regulations; at six, the press conference began; at 6:53, Schabowski said "effective immediately"; at 7:04, dpa sent its flash; at 7:17, ZDF broadcast it; at eight, the first channel led with it; around ten, the crowds surged toward the checkpoints; at half past eleven, the barrier at Bornholmer Straße went up. Within a single day, an internal document went from a slip of paper to television news, from television news to crowds in the streets, and from crowds to a raised barrier.

There were no heroes that day, and no conspiracy. Schabowski did not misspeak on purpose — he truly had not understood the note in his hand. Jäger did not disobey on purpose — he truly did not know what else to do. Krenz did not set the people free on purpose — he truly wanted to buy time. East Germany's leadership collectively lost control of the situation that night, not because anyone had plotted anything, but because everyone was waiting for someone else to decide first.

On October 3, 1990, Germany was formally reunified. Less than a year after that night.

Epilogue: Did One Slip of the Tongue Rewrite History?

The fall of the Berlin Wall is often told as the story of "one slip of the tongue that ended the Cold War." The telling is very gripping, but it tells only half the story. The slip was real, and the accident was real — but the accident could take effect only because everything underneath had already come loose: Hungary had opened its border, and tens of thousands had already voted with their feet; Honecker had fallen, and the government had changed hands again and again; half a million people had taken to the streets; and Gorbachev in the Soviet Union had made it clear that he would not intervene with troops. The wall was no longer a solid wall; only the look of one remained, and Schabowski's sentence was no more than the last push.

History has many such moments: it looks as though someone, on some day, said one wrong sentence or made one wrong decision, and the world turned upside down. Look closely, and behind it there is always a string of conditions long since in place. The accident does the igniting; necessity supplies the firewood. On the night the Berlin Wall fell, the man who struck the match was a spokesman who had not read his note — and the ones who supplied the firewood were the tens of thousands of people who, in the months before, had crossed the border, crowded into the embassy, and walked onto the square.
