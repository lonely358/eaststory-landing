Kangxi Traps Oboi: How a Sixteen-Year-Old Emperor Won Back His Power with a Wrestling Game

Palace Intrigues · Nov 18, 2026 · ~11 min read

On May 16, 1669, something seemingly trivial happened inside the Forbidden City: the sixteen-year-old Kangxi Emperor, as he often did, summoned a band of young guards to practise buku, the Manchu art of wrestling, in the palace.

That day, the powerful minister Oboi entered the palace for an audience, as he always did. At a single word from the emperor, the wrestling boys rushed forward together, bore Oboi to the ground, and seized him.

The whole thing took no longer than a cup of tea. The sky over the Great Qing had changed.

This is the affair later known as "Kangxi cleverly captures Oboi." A sixteen-year-old emperor, with a wrestling game he had played for two years, took back the empire that was rightfully his.

I. An Eight-Year-Old Emperor and Four Regents

First, turn the clock back eight years. In 1661 the Shunzhi Emperor died, aged only twenty-four, of smallpox. On his deathbed he passed the throne to his third son, Xuanye — the future Kangxi Emperor.

There was a very practical reason Xuanye could be chosen: as a small child he had caught smallpox and survived it, which left him immune. The missionary Tang Ruowang (Johann Adam Schall von Bell) advised Shunzhi to appoint a prince who had already had smallpox, so that the throne would sit steady. Shunzhi took the advice.

When Xuanye came to the throne he was only eight years old by the traditional count. An eight-year-old child could not govern an empire. Shunzhi's dying edict named four regents to assist him: Sonin, Suksaha, Ebilun, and Oboi.

Of the four, Sonin was the oldest and the most senior; Suksaha had been a trusted confidant of Shunzhi himself; Ebilun was of illustrious birth but had no mind of his own; Oboi was a Manchu general risen by military merit — a first-rate fighter, and the hardest character of them all.

By design, the four were meant to check one another, governing jointly until the emperor came of age. But institutions are dead things, and men are alive. Sonin, old and sickly, died in 1667, and of the three who remained, Oboi's fist was the hardest and his nerve the largest. Ebilun, having no opinions of his own, gradually attached himself to Oboi. Suksaha tried to contend — and paid for it with his life.

Before long, the government of the empire had become one man's word. That man was Oboi.

II. How Oboi Came to Where He Stood

Oboi had not always been an overmighty minister. In his youth he was a fighting man of genuine, hard-won merit.

He came from the Manchu Bordered Yellow Banner and had campaigned with Hong Taiji; he won great distinction in the Songjin campaign, the battles around Songshan and Jinzhou. After Hong Taiji's death he was caught up for a time in the struggle for power and was suppressed by Dorgon. But he endured, and under Shunzhi he rose steadily to First-Class Duke and Deliberative Minister.

The trouble began after he held power. After Sonin's death, Oboi grew ever more high-handed. Around 1667, using the "land enclosure" affair as his opening, he successively had the Minister of Revenue Sunahai, the Governor-General of Zhili Zhu Changzuo, and the Governor Wang Denglian put to death. The enclosures meant forcibly seizing Han Chinese land for the Eight Banners; Sunahai and the others lost their lives because they opposed the enclosures and stood up to Oboi. Then he framed his fellow regent Suksaha on false charges and had him condemned to death.

He killed colleagues, he killed great frontier officials, and he killed without blinking. High and low throughout the court, every man feared for himself.

In 1667 the fourteen-year-old Kangxi formally assumed personal rule. By the rules, personal rule meant the emperor's own word was law. But Oboi was still there, and the great power was still in his hands. Edicts Kangxi issued often could not be carried out once they reached Oboi. What the emperor wanted done, Oboi could veto with a single sentence.

A fourteen-year-old emperor and a minister who held the armies in his grip and killed without blinking, living under the same roofs of the Forbidden City. How long could such a state of affairs last?

III. The Two-Year Wrestling Game

Kangxi did not meet force with force. He chose a method no one could have foreseen: he played along with Oboi.

Every day he summoned a group of young guards into the palace to practise buku. Buku was the traditional Manchu wrestling game — two lads locked together, grappling — at once a pastime and a martial exercise. Kangxi took to the floor himself and trained with the boys.

Oboi watched, and took it for a child's amusement. A teenaged emperor, wrestling all day with a pack of half-grown boys — what could ever come of that? His mind was set at ease; he even felt that this little emperor was nothing much after all.

What Oboi did not know was that Kangxi played this "game" for two years.

In those two years the buku boys were replaced batch after batch, every one of them picked by Kangxi personally — strong of body, quick of hand. What they drilled every day was not only the holds and throws of wrestling, but the instinct to move as one at a command. One order from the emperor, and every man would lunge at the same instant; even a strong man could not break free.

At the same time, Kangxi was quietly doing something else: drawing reliable officials of the court to his side. Wang Hongzuo, Minister of War, and Huang Xigun, Left Vice Minister of War, were both secretly brought in, charged with seizing control of the capital's troops on the day of the strike, to keep Oboi's partisans from raising trouble.

On the surface, Kangxi's days went on as usual: court every day, study, martial practice, not a hint of anything amiss. When Oboi came into the palace for an audience, he dealt with him smiling, as ever. The closer the day came, the more he acted as if nothing were happening.

In the palace there was also his grandmother, the Grand Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang, holding the centre. Xiaozhuang had weathered the storms of two reigns, and she watched every step Kangxi took. Grandmother and grandson: one performed on the stage, the other stood guard behind it.

This game of chess Kangxi played for two years. Oboi was in the light, Kangxi in the shadow. Oboi thought he was watching a play. In fact, he was the man inside it.

IV. The Day

May 16, 1669 — the sixteenth day of the fifth month of the eighth year of Kangxi.

Oboi entered the palace for his audience as usual. He walked into the hall, performed the ceremonies and presented his business as he always did, noticing nothing out of the ordinary. The buku boys waited outside the hall, exactly as on any other day.

Kangxi suddenly spoke, enumerated Oboi's crimes of "gross disrespect," and ordered his arrest. The boys surged forward and fell upon Oboi. Oboi was an old general who had won an empire from horseback, but two fists cannot beat four hands — still less when the hands belonged to boys who had trained for two years. After a brief struggle, he was pinned to the ground.

In no more than the time of a cup of tea, Oboi, who had dominated the court for eight years, was a prisoner at the foot of the throne.

Afterwards the ministers deliberated on Oboi's offences and drew up a list of thirty counts, requesting that he and his clan be exterminated. Kangxi, mindful that Oboi in his early years had saved the life of his grandfather Hong Taiji, remitted the death sentence and changed it to imprisonment for life. Many of Oboi's brothers, nephews, attendants, and partisans were executed. The other regent, Ebilun, for his long collusion with Oboi, was stripped of his titles of Grand Preceptor and First-Class Duke.

From that day on, the sixteen-year-old Kangxi truly began to rule in person.

V. What This Lesson Is Worth

The story of "Kangxi cleverly captures Oboi" has been told for more than three hundred years. In the popular retellings there are picturesque details — a chair leg sawn through in advance, a bowl of scalding tea — told with great colour. But these details do not appear in the official histories; they were added by later novels and operas. They are fine as entertainment; they will not do as fact.

What the official histories can confirm is this much: the buku boys were real, the arrest was real, the thirty counts were real, and the remission of the death sentence to imprisonment was real. Oboi died in his place of confinement; the exact time and cause of his death are not recorded in detail in the official histories.

What is truly worth pondering in this story is not how thrilling the moment of the capture was, but the two years before it.

A sixteen-year-old emperor, facing a minister who killed without blinking, did not throw himself into a rash confrontation and did not show his hand too early. He held his nerve and played wrestling with his opponent for two years. In those two years he trained up a squad that would move at his word, won over officials he could trust, and learned his opponent through and through; only when the day came that nothing could go wrong did he strike.

Look at it from the other side: where did Oboi lose? He lost by underestimating his enemy. He took the emperor's "play" for weakness and his youth for incompetence. An old general who held the armies in his fist lost in the end to a band of wrestling boys — and what he lost was not strength but judgement. To his dying day he probably never understood it: the little emperor who wrestled with the boys every day was never thinking about wrestling at all.

One more point is often overlooked: before Kangxi struck, Oboi actually had chances to notice. The buku had gone on for two years, the boys changing batch after batch, and people inside and outside the palace were watching. It was not that Oboi never heard of it; he heard, and thought nothing of it. The greatest failing of an overmighty minister is the conviction that he sees through everything.

After Kangxi took personal rule he reigned sixty-one years, pacified the Three Feudatories, recovered Taiwan, and signed the Treaty of Nerchinsk, opening the Qing dynasty's age of greatness. The Draft History of Qing judges him thus: "He inherited the great enterprise early; diligent in government and loving toward his people, versed in letters and in war, he brought all under heaven into one." The starting point of those sixty-one years was the space of a cup of tea on May 16, 1669.

Among the first things he did after taking power was to right a wrong. In Oboi's years of dominance, the astronomical official Yang Guangxian had colluded with Oboi against Western learning and manufactured the "Calendar Case," which got the missionary Tang Ruowang sentenced to death. In the eighth month of the very year Kangxi seized Oboi, he rehabilitated Tang Ruowang. It was not a large matter, but the signal was clear: the unjust cases of the Oboi era would be overturned by the emperor one by one.

Epilogue: After the Day at Sixteen

People who tell the story of Kangxi like to begin with "an emperor for the ages," and speak of how wise and mighty he was. But an emperor for the ages is not born; he was fought into being, step by step, starting from a wrestling game on the day he was sixteen.

Before that day, he was a puppet emperor in name only. After that day, he truly became Kangxi.

History is full of young sovereigns who fell into the hands of powerful ministers. Kangxi did not fall. What saved him was not luck, but two years of patience, and decisiveness at the moment of the strike.

Patience that can afford to wait, and a hand that dares to come down. Lacking either one, the story in the Forbidden City on May 16, 1669 would have been written very differently.

Of course, Kangxi had his shortcomings too. In his later years, immersed in the achievements of his first half-century, he grew weary of government; administration gradually decayed, and the deposition of the crown prince set off decades of struggle among his sons for the succession, leaving a heap of problems for Yongzheng after him. All that came later, and it does not diminish the worth of that day in 1669. For a sixteen-year-old emperor to lay his trap for two years under a powerful minister's nose, and to close the net in a single day at the end of it — that is a rare gift in any dynasty.
