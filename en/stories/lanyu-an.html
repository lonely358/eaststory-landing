The Lan Yu Case: Five Years After Crushing the Northern Yuan at Lake Buyur, 15,000 People Were Buried with Him

Palace Intrigues · Nov 19, 2026 · ~12 min read

In the spring of 1388, Lan Yu led an army of 150,000 deep into the northern desert and at Lake Buyur fought the most brilliant victory of the Ming dynasty since its founding. When the dispatch reached Nanjing, Zhu Yuanzhang was so delighted that he meant to make Lan Yu Duke of Liang. Five years later, the same emperor ordered Lan Yu executed, his clan exterminated to the third degree, and 15,000 people implicated and destroyed with him. A man went from god of war to traitor in five years. What exactly was the emperor afraid of? The account has to be reckoned from the beginning.

I. An Army of 150,000 Marches Beyond the Frontier

Lan Yu was the younger brother-in-law of Chang Yuchun, Prince of Kaiping, and had campaigned in Chang's tent from his early years; the histories say of him that "facing the enemy he was brave, and wherever he went he prevailed." He followed Fu Youde into Sichuan, followed Xu Da against the Northern Yuan, followed Mu Ying to subdue Tibet, and rose on his victories all the way to Marquis of Yongchang. In the campaign against Nahachu in 1387, he was already the commander in all but name.

In the third month of 1388, Zhu Yuanzhang formally appointed Lan Yu Grand General and ordered him to lead 150,000 men against the Northern Yuan emperor, Togus Temur. The Ming army drove deep into the desert; near Lake Buyur, with the enemy still nowhere to be seen forty li away (a li is about 0.5 km), Lan Yu nearly gave the order to withdraw. Wang Bi, Marquis of Dingyuan, talked him out of it: to lead well over a hundred thousand men this deep into the desert and go home empty-handed — how would he answer for it? Lan Yu listened. He ordered the whole army to cook its meals at night in pits dug in the ground, so that no smoke or fire showed, and under cover of a wind that raised the sand, crept up to the very edge of the Yuan camp and destroyed it in one stroke.

The tally of that battle stands clearly written in the History of Ming: the Northern Yuan emperor's second son Dibaonu captured, along with more than a hundred imperial consorts, princesses, and others; more than 77,000 men and women taken; the imperial seal of state, gold and silver seals, and more than 150,000 horses, camels, cattle, and sheep seized. The Northern Yuan emperor escaped with only a few dozen horsemen and never recovered from the blow. When the dispatch reached Nanjing the whole country rejoiced, and Zhu Yuanzhang issued an edict of praise, saying Lan Yu could stand comparison with Wei Qing of Han and Li Jing of Tang.

Lan Yu had taken seventeen years to reach this point. In 1371 he followed Fu Youde into Sichuan and took Mianzhou; the next year he followed Xu Da north and defeated the Yuan army at the Tula River; in 1381, as Left Deputy General of the Southern Expedition, he went with Fu Youde into Yunnan and captured Dalima, the Yuan's chief administrator there — Yunnan was pacified, and the greatest merit was his. In 1387, in the campaign against Nahachu, he led light cavalry through heavy snow in a surprise attack, killing and capturing enemy commanders, until Nahachu finally surrendered. When the commander-in-chief Feng Sheng fell into disgrace and had his general's seal taken away, Lan Yu was appointed Grand General on the spot, in the army itself. From a battalion-level military commissioner to Grand General, Lan Yu had relied on victory after victory; his seniority and his record were there for all to see, and no one could find fault with them.

II. From Duke of Liang the Beam to Duke of Liang the Cold

Then the conferring of rewards went wrong. Zhu Yuanzhang had originally intended to enfeoff Lan Yu as Duke of Liang — Liang written with the character for "beam." At the last moment he changed his mind, and enfeoffed him only as Duke of Liang — Liang written with the character for "cold." One character's difference, and a world of meaning between them. According to the History of Ming, the reason for the change was that Lan Yu, while with the army, had "privately taken the Yuan ruler's consort" — he had seized a captured consort of the Northern Yuan for himself; the woman killed herself in shame, Zhu Yuanzhang was furious, and he had this fault inscribed on Lan Yu's hereditary iron certificate.

The episode speaks volumes about the relationship between the two men. Zhu Yuanzhang admired Lan Yu's gift for war, but from the very beginning he had no intention of indulging him. Lan Yu, for his part, showed no restraint. After his triumphant return to court he seized commoners' fields at Dongchang, and when a censor came to investigate, he drove the censor away. Returning from the northern campaign, he reached the Xifeng Pass at night; when the pass officer would not open the gate, he simply had his soldiers smash the gate and force their way in. Speaking before the emperor he was arrogant, and resentful that he had been made only Tutor to the Crown Prince, ranking below the Duke of Song and the Duke of Ying, he complained openly: "Am I not fit to be Grand Preceptor?" The History of Ming sums him up as "growing ever more arrogant, wilful, and unrestrained" — the greater his merit, the less he cared for the rules.

What a founding emperor fears most is precisely a general who holds great armies and holds the rules in contempt. Every one of these faults of Lan Yu's, Zhu Yuanzhang noted down, stroke by stroke.

III. Once the Crown Prince Died, the Wind Changed

Lan Yu's greatest support was the Crown Prince, Zhu Biao. Lan Yu was a relation of the Crown Prince by marriage — his niece, Lady Chang, was the Crown Princess — and the two households were close. On that return from campaign, Lan Yu had privately said a fateful thing to the Crown Prince: the Prince of Yan, Zhu Di, conducted himself in his fief exactly like an emperor, and, he had heard, the physiognomists and watchers of celestial signs said the land of Yan carried the aura of a Son of Heaven; would the Crown Prince please take precautions early. Zhu Biao did not take it seriously; he answered that the Prince of Yan had always been respectful to him and that there was nothing of the kind. The words later reached Zhu Di's ears, and became a knot between Lan Yu and the Prince of Yan that could never be untied.

In the fourth month of 1392, the Crown Prince Zhu Biao died of illness. Zhu Yuanzhang made Zhu Biao's eldest son by a secondary consort, Zhu Yunwen, the Imperial Grandson-Heir; Zhu Yunwen was fifteen years old at the time. With the Crown Prince dead, Lan Yu's position changed completely. Before, he had been the Crown Prince's kinsman, the military pillar of the heir's party; now the heir was a young grandson, and in the new sovereign's eyes a veteran general like Lan Yu — holding military power and intractable by nature — turned from a pillar into a hidden danger.

At this time Zhu Di also came to court and submitted a memorial, and he spoke bluntly: the dukes and marquises at court were running wild and breaking the law; in time they would grow too big to be controlled, and they should be dealt with while there was still time. The words were aimed at Lan Yu, and Zhu Yuanzhang took them in. Less than a year after the Crown Prince's death, Zhu Yuanzhang was already preparing to raise another great purge and clear out the meritorious officials.

IV. Jiang Huan's Single Sheet of Accusation

In the second month of 1393, Jiang Huan, commander of the Embroidered Uniform Guard, accused Lan Yu of plotting rebellion, saying he meant to strike when the emperor performed the ritual ploughing of the sacred field. Zhu Yuanzhang received the accusation and that very day ordered Lan Yu executed, his property confiscated, and his clan exterminated to the third degree. Executed in the same case were Cao Zhen, Marquis of Jingchuan; Zhang Yi, Marquis of Heqing; Zhu Shou, Marquis of Zhulu; He Rong, Earl of Dongguan; and also Zhan Hui, Minister of Personnel, and Fu Youwen, Vice Minister of Revenue, among others. Zhu Yuanzhang had the confessions in the case compiled into the Record of Treasonous Ministers and proclaimed to the empire.

It is worth asking one more question about where those confessions came from. The so-called evidence of rebellion consisted mainly of Jiang Huan's accusation and the depositions taken in prison. Whether Lan Yu truly intended rebellion — on this, historians generally hold the evidence to be very thin. The more persuasive explanation is that this was a political purge: the emperor meant to clear the road for his young Imperial Grandson-Heir, and Lan Yu happened to be standing in the line of fire. This has to be stated plainly: the "rebellion" cannot be written down as established fact.

As for the "flaying and stuffing with straw" that popular accounts often mention — its source is the Shu jing lu (A Warning Record of Shu) of the Qing dynasty, which says that when Zhang Xianzhong fought his way into the Prince of Shu's palace in 1644, he saw on the gate tower of Duanli Gate a portrait of Lan Yu made from his skin. That is a record from more than two hundred years later, not an archive of the early Ming; it has to be cited as such, and cannot be treated as an on-the-scene record of the time.

V. Fifteen Thousand People

The final number of the Lan Yu case stands in the History of Ming: 15,000 people were executed as his kin and adherents. Named in the Record of Treasonous Ministers were one duke, thirteen marquises, and two earls. The case was not over: Fu Youde, Duke of Ying, Feng Sheng, Duke of Song, and a whole batch of founding veterans, in the following year or two, killed themselves one after another or were put to death. By this time the founding officials of the early Ming had been killed almost to a man; only a very few, such as Tang He and Geng Bingwen, remained.

Fu Youde's death shows Zhu Yuanzhang's mind especially clearly. Fu Youde was a founding general of the same rank as Lan Yu and Feng Sheng; not long after the Lan Yu case broke, Zhu Yuanzhang publicly humiliated him at a banquet, and Fu Youde went home and killed himself. Feng Sheng was recalled to the capital and granted death. Tang He survived by handing over his military power early and retiring to his home district to live out his years; Geng Bingwen survived by being cautious and timid in all things and never forming a faction. Of the same generation of founding officials, those who lived were all men who knew when to withdraw their hand.

Of those 15,000 people, the overwhelming majority had nothing whatsoever to do with any rebellion. They were Lan Yu's clansmen, his subordinates, his colleagues, his disciples; touch the slightest connection and you were swept in. In the ninth month Zhu Yuanzhang issued an edict saying that from then on the Hu faction and the Lan faction were all pardoned and would be pursued no further. The pardon came too late. The people were already killed.

Lan Yu's home district had its own sequel. Around Chengbu in Hunan, his homeland, residents surnamed Lan, fearing implication, changed their surname to Qin; the Lans of Dingyuan in Anhui changed theirs to Qing. One case changed the surnames of whole districts.

Epilogue: How an Emperor Reckons a Meritorious Servant's Account

The first half of Lan Yu's life is the textbook road of a great general: born into a military household, victorious again and again, enfeoffed as a duke in his thirties, destroyer of the Northern Yuan's main strength. The second half is the textbook case of merit so great it shakes the sovereign: arrogant on the strength of his achievements, careless of the rules, and the moment his support fell, he became a thorn in the emperor's eye.

When Zhu Yuanzhang killed Lan Yu, he was not killing a man who had rebelled; he was killing a man who might threaten the succession of the throne. While the Crown Prince Zhu Biao lived, Lan Yu's arrogance could be tolerated, because the Crown Prince could hold him down. Once the Crown Prince was dead, the fifteen-year-old Imperial Grandson-Heir could not hold him down — and so Lan Yu had to die. The emperor reckoned this account very clearly, and very coldly.

So the true lesson of the Lan Yu case is not on the battlefield but at court. The skill of war can enfeoff a man as duke or marquis; it cannot keep him alive. What keeps a man alive is a sense of proportion toward power. And that was precisely what Lan Yu lacked.

Nor was the Lan Yu case an isolated event. There were four great cases of the Hongwu reign in all: the Hu Weiyong case, the Empty Seal case, the Guo Huan case, and the Lan Yu case — known to history as the Four Great Cases of Hongwu. The four together killed tens of thousands of people. With these four great purges Zhu Yuanzhang dismantled the founding officials' bloc completely and gathered all power back into the hands of the emperor alone. Lan Yu was the last of them, and the largest closing of the account.
