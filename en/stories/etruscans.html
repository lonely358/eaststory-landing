The Etruscans: The Teachers Rome Erased from History

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~8 min read

Rome's earliest kings spoke Etruscan.

In the sixth century BC, Rome was still a growing city on the banks of the Tiber, and it was ruled by Etruscan kings. The Harvard historian Michael McCormick has said that the Etruscans exercised a formative influence on early Rome, and that many of Rome's institutions and customs were borrowed from this neighbor, or adapted from theirs. Some scholars have even argued that the city of Rome itself was founded by Etruscans.

These people called themselves the Rasenna. They lived in west-central Italy, between the Arno and the Tiber, their heartland a league of twelve city-states. The Greeks called them the Tyrrhenians, and the Tyrrhenian Sea off Italy's western coast takes its name from them. The Romans called them the Etrusci, a word that survived as a place name and is with us still: Tuscany.

The things Rome took from its teachers, laid out, make a long list.

First, writing. The Latin alphabet was not conjured out of nothing by the Romans: the Greek alphabet passed first into Etruscan hands, was reshaped by them, and only then reached the Latins. Next, the skeleton of the city. Etruscan practices in roads, water supply, and drainage were already mature before the Romans carried them further, and Roman city planning had Etruscan precedents. And then the most elaborate craft of all: divination. The Etruscans built divination into an entire discipline, which the Romans called the *disciplina etrusca*, sorted into categories — some read the livers of sacrificial animals, some read lightning, some governed the founding of cities and the conduct of ritual. The discipline was written down in several books: the Books of the Liver governed how to read a sacrificial liver; the Books of Lightning, how to interpret thunderbolts by their position in the sky; the Ritual Books ranged widest of all, covering everything from the rites for founding a city to the rules of social life. Rome's priestly ranks long kept a place for Etruscan diviners, whose word was sought on affairs of state. Whenever the Roman state faced a great matter, it summoned Etruscan priests to read livers and omens, from the age of the kings into the age of the emperors, for centuries without a break. Some scholars have also pointed out that the triumph and the gladiatorial games trace their origins to the Etruscans as well.

They were superb metalworkers and merchants, selling their goods all over the Mediterranean. Their tombs often hold pottery imported from Greece, alongside pieces in which they had learned the Greek manner and bent it to their own taste.

Greek and Roman authors always wrote about Etruscan women with a certain unease: at banquets they reclined and drank beside their husbands, they appeared openly in public, and their standing was far higher than that of women in the Greek city-states. To their neighbors, this people seemed strange even in the habits of daily life.

And yet this people, which taught Rome, left no history of its own. What we know of them today comes not from their books but from their tombs.

The problem lies in the language. Etruscan is related to none of the Indo-European languages around it; it is an orphan. Some thirteen thousand inscriptions survive — which sounds like a great deal, except that the overwhelming majority are short phrases on tombstones and votive offerings: a name, a year, to whom it was dedicated. Longer texts can be counted on one's fingers. Their alphabet derives from the Greek one, and scholars broadly know the sound of each letter, so the inscriptions can be "read aloud" — but what the words add up to is still, for the most part, guesswork.

Their writing also ran against the later European grain: they wrote from right to left. The alphabet had twenty-six letters, but a few of them were created and then scarcely used, because Etruscan had no voiced consonants like b, d, and g; for the sound f, on the other hand, they invented a new letter, which later entered the Latin alphabet as well. Latin still keeps dozens of Etruscan loanwords; some scholars believe that words like *populus* (people), *tuba* (trumpet), and *columna* (column) all have their roots in Etruscan.

Linguists have found the language two distant relatives. In 1886, a stele unearthed on the Greek island of Lemnos bore a text clearly of the same family as Etruscan; the Raetic language of the Alps is also thought to be akin to it. In 1998, the linguist Helmut Rix grouped these three languages together as the Tyrsenian family. The proposal has won acceptance from many scholars, though the surviving material for all three languages is so scant that the kinship can only be called broadly established, impossible to prove in any depth.

The material that can help is pitifully scarce. In 1964, archaeologists at Pyrgi in Italy dug up several gold tablets inscribed with a bilingual text in Etruscan and Phoenician, and only by way of the Phoenician half were scholars able to match up a batch of word meanings. The Etruscans once had a rich literature — Roman authors mention it — but exactly one book of theirs survives, the *Liber Linteus*, the Linen Book, written on flax cloth. The way it survived is absurd: the cloth later found its way to Egypt, where it was cut into strips and wound, round and round, as bandages around a mummy. The book is now kept in Zagreb, Croatia, and most of its contents still cannot be read.

Where they came from in the first place, the ancients themselves argued about. In the fifth century BC, Herodotus said they were Lydians from Asia Minor who, in a time of famine, sent half their population overseas to settle in Italy. Dionysius, who came later, disagreed, holding that this people was native to Italy and like no one else. Neither side ever persuaded the other; the argument ran for more than two thousand years.

In 2021, a genetic study published in *Science Advances* brought new evidence. A team led by Cosimo Posth at the University of Tübingen sequenced the DNA of dozens of remains from the Etruscan period, and the conclusion was somewhat surprising: genetically, the Etruscans were indistinguishable from their Latin neighbors, alike descended from Stone Age farmers and later steppe migrants. In Posth's phrase, they were "indistinguishable from the Latins." The oddity is this: around 1600 BC the steppe peoples brought Indo-European languages with them, and almost everywhere in Europe the new language overwhelmed the old — except in Tuscany, where the newcomers learned the local tongue and married local people, and the old language and culture survived instead. As the researchers put it, everywhere else the new culture won; here, it did not.

The more likely picture, then, is this: Etruscan was one of Europe's old languages from before the Indo-European arrival, washed away everywhere else long ago, holding out only in this one place — and holding out for eight hundred years, until Rome's Latin finally pushed it out of history.

The pushing-out was not quick. In 396 BC, the great Etruscan city of Veii fell after a ten-year Roman siege, and over the following centuries the remaining city-states were swallowed up and assimilated one after another. Politically the Etruscans were finished, yet the language lived on in the countryside for several more centuries, with inscriptions still being carved into the mid-first century AD. The last known person who could read Etruscan was the Roman emperor Claudius. His first wife, Urgulanilla, was herself Etruscan. He genuinely labored over this dying language: he sought out the last elderly country folk who could still speak it, compiled a dictionary, and wrote a twenty-volume *History of the Etruscans*. The dictionary and those twenty volumes were all later lost. Around AD 180, the Latin writer Aulus Gellius was still mentioning Etruscan in passing in his essays, pairing it with Gaulish, another language then dying out. By that time, the only people who could read the script were a handful of antiquarian scholars.

The image they left to later ages lives mostly in tomb murals and sarcophagi. The most famous piece is the Sarcophagus of the Spouses: a husband and wife reclining side by side on a banqueting couch, the wife taking up exactly as much space as the husband, their manner intimate. There is also the Tomb of the Reliefs, its walls and pillars carved with reliefs of everyday household goods, as if an entire family's house had been moved underground. From these tombs one can see that their women truly were unlike the neighbors': even in death, they were to sit level with their husbands.

The student learned everything the teacher had, and the textbook was renamed for the student. In life, the Etruscans built their cities on hilltops, and the Romans afterward built upward, layer upon layer, on their foundations, so that little of Etruscan work survives above ground. It is the tombs they built for their dead that remain — the murals, the terracotta figures, the sarcophagi of husbands and wives buried together. Most of what we know of this people today has been taken out of their graves, piece by piece.
