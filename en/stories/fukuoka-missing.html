The Missing Employees Were Buried in the Yard of the Boss's Family Home: The Chikugo Recycling Shop Case

Deep Dives · Nov 25, 2026 · ~9 min read

In April 2014, police in Japan's Fukuoka Prefecture arrested a couple who ran a recycling shop — the husband 47, the wife 45. The charge was a small one: using an acquaintance's consumer-finance card to withdraw cash. In the interrogation room, the husband, Nakao Shinya, volunteered something that had nothing to do with theft — the bodies of former employees of the shop were buried in the yard of his family home; later they had been dug up, the bones smashed, and the pieces thrown into a river.

The police dug where he told them to dig. In the yard and along the river, they really did find fragments of human bone. DNA testing showed that some of them belonged to a young man named Hidaka Takashi. He had been "missing" for ten full years.

Nakao Shinya and his wife Nakao Chisa opened their recycling shop in April 2003 in Chikugo, Fukuoka Prefecture. According to later Japanese media reports, the two had met at a club in Fukuoka where Chisa worked as a hostess and Shinya was a customer; after the marriage, it was always the wife who had the final say. Neither of them had had an easy life before that: Shinya had dropped out of high school and drifted through a string of jobs, and had filed for bankruptcy in 1998; Chisa had run a coffee shop that closed after nine months, and she went bankrupt in 2001. The money to open the recycling shop — twenty million yen — was borrowed from acquaintances, and they paid it back over several years. The shop itself had hardly any customers, yet the couple wore expensive suits and drove luxury cars, and by all appearances lived quite well.

The shop sold second-hand goods and employed a few young workers. According to the management style recorded in the court's later judgment, this is how the shop ran: the couple came in only about once a week, but every ten minutes an employee had to telephone Chisa to report on the progress of his work. Reporting by the Japanese outlet NETIB-NEWS on the trial added more detail: working hours ran from nine in the morning until two the next morning, with more than fifty reports a day, and a thousand yen docked from wages for a single mistake; the standard for cleaning was that not one grain of sand, not one hair, not one water stain could be left behind. Mistakes were punished with beatings — and the boss did not do the beating herself. The employees beat each other. Whoever had erred had to ask a colleague, out loud, to slap him across the face, and the colleague would oblige. When Chisa was not there, the employees inspected one another, and the beatings went on just the same.

In a shop like that, two people were beaten more than anyone else. One was Hidaka Takashi, who had come to work there in August 2003, aged 22. The other was a male employee who had started a few months before him and was 19 at the time of the case; the Japanese reports never printed his name.

In December 2003, the two were taken to live in the Nakaos' apartment. According to NETIB-NEWS, the last time Hidaka's mother ever saw her son was on December 24 of that year. From then on, the two young men ate and slept under the bosses' eyes; work done badly meant a beating, and sometimes no food at all. As time passed, their faces were so swollen with bruises that they could not be seen by customers, so they were kept in the apartment washing merchandise and no longer appeared at the shop. Trial reporting said they had wasted away to the point where walking at a normal pace was difficult. Even so, neither of them ran. The judgment later recorded that the court, in its deliberations, had specifically discussed one question: whether, at that point, they had actually had any possibility of escape.

In April 2004, Hidaka's mother came to the shop wanting to see her son. The couple refused, and told her Hidaka owed them seven million yen, demanding that she repay it for him. The mother paid the money off in installments, then asked again to see him. Shinya handed her a mobile phone with a call in progress. On the other end, her son said only one thing: "I'm fine." It was the last time she ever heard his voice.

Between late May and early June 2004, the 19-year-old employee died first. According to the judgment, Chisa told Shinya when he arrived that Hidaka had beaten the young man to death, and Hidaka admitted it. The two of them discussed whether to stop the beatings and send Hidaka home. In the end, they did not stop. Chisa told Hidaka again and again that the boy's death was his responsibility. Hidaka himself was afraid of being arrested, and never dared say a word to anyone outside.

In late June 2004, after yet another beating, Hidaka Takashi died. From the day he moved into that apartment, he had never once gone home.

That July, Hidaka's family filed a missing-person report with the Chikugo police station, and afterwards went in to ask about it three more times. Nothing came of it. Their son did not return, and no one told them where he had gone.

The shop carried on as usual. In March 2006, Chisa's younger sister and her brother-in-law, Hiyamizu Kazuya, then 33, came to work at the shop. According to the judgment, the couple first told the other employees that Hiyamizu was putting on airs, trading on his ability and experience, and isolated him. Then he was shut alone into a small room inside the office and beaten with a shoehorn and a golf club. In August 2006, Chisa's sister left her sister's house, leaving behind her four-year-old son, Daito. A week later the sister came back, but Daito remained at the couple's apartment. The Nakaos took the boy into their own home to "look after" him. Between September and October of that year, Hiyamizu Kazuya died from the continuing beatings; Daito, too, died sometime between late September and early October. In its indictment, the prosecution said the couple, in the name of "discipline," had repeatedly beaten the defenseless four-year-old about the head and face.

Where the four bodies went was something Shinya himself revealed ten years later: buried in the yard of his family home, left until they had skeletonized, then dug up, the bones crushed and scattered into a river. When the police searched in 2014, they found some human bones in the yard and in the river, and DNA matched two of the employees, Hidaka among them. Daito's remains were never found.

In September 2012, the Chikugo city government had consulted the Fukuoka prefectural police because Daito's whereabouts were unknown. After the case broke, Japanese media at one point reported that five or six people around the couple were unaccounted for. But the deaths that completed the judicial process and were recognized by the court numbered four: Hidaka Takashi, the 19-year-old employee, Hiyamizu Kazuya, and Daito. For the 19-year-old, the statute of limitations on causing death by injury had already expired, so there was no indictment; Chisa was also implicated in the death by injury of an acquaintance in Fukuoka City around 1994, which likewise was only referred to prosecutors without indictment because of the time limit.

On April 11, 2014, the couple were arrested for theft. On June 16, police announced that DNA from bone fragments found in the yard matched Hidaka Takashi, and arrested the two again on suspicion of murder; they were rearrested in August over Hiyamizu's case and in September over Daito's. Daito's case had no remains; it was pieced together from confessions and other evidence. The prosecution charged murder in Hidaka's case, and causing death by injury in the cases of Hiyamizu and his son.

In court, the couple pushed the blame onto each other. Shinya said he had long lived under his wife's psychological domination and had used his hands only on her instructions; he admitted causing Hiyamizu's death by injury and denied most of the rest. Chisa said Hidaka had died as a result of a beating by her husband after Shinya scolded Hidaka for hitting their daughter on the head, that the husband was the one who did the beating, that she had given no instructions, and she pleaded not guilty to murder and to causing death by injury. One sentence spoken in court by the couple's eldest daughter was quoted by many Japanese outlets: "What Mom says is absolute. In our house, Mom is Gian, Dad is Suneo, and there is no Doraemon."

The lay-judge trial at the Fukuoka District Court ultimately found that all three prosecuted deaths constituted causing death by injury, not murder — on the grounds that the evidence did not show violence forceful enough to prove an intent to kill. The prosecution objected, arguing that Chisa had at least acted with "indirect intent" — that is, she knew that beating them like this might kill them, and beat them anyway. The Fukuoka High Court, on appeal, did not accept that argument either, holding that the evidence was insufficient to prove her awareness of the fatal outcome had reached that degree. The prosecution had sought life imprisonment for both. On June 24, 2016, the court sentenced Chisa to thirty years, finding that she was the one who had designed and directed the whole framework of the crimes. On August 8 of the same year, Shinya was sentenced to twenty-eight years; the court credited him with cooperating in clarifying the case and with showing remorse, but rejected the claim that he had been dominated by his wife, finding that he had acted on his own judgment. In January 2017, the Fukuoka High Court dismissed both sides' appeals; Chisa did not appeal further, and her thirty years stood. In March of that year, Shinya's conviction was upheld on appeal, and his twenty-eight years became final in April.

Hidaka Takashi's family went to the police station three times to report him missing. In the end, this case came to light not because of those three reports. It came to light because, ten years later, the couple misused someone else's consumer-finance card — and because, in the interrogation room, the husband opened his mouth himself.
