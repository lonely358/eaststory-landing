The Rosetta Stone: A Black Stone That Taught a Language Dead for 1,400 Years to Speak Again

World Curiosities · Nov 19, 2026 · ~10 min read

On July 15, 1799, a few French soldiers building fortifications near the town of Rosetta, at the mouth of the Nile in Egypt, struck a black stone with their spades. The stone was covered with writing, and no one could read it. They reported it up, and the officer who saw it knew at once that this was no ordinary thing. No one imagined that this stone would teach a language dead for 1,400 years to speak again, and let mankind read ancient Egypt anew.

I. A Black Stone Dug Up While Building a Fort

When the stone was discovered, Napoleon's expeditionary army was at war in Egypt. Rosetta was a port city on the western side of the Nile Delta, and the French were enlarging Fort Julien there. Captain Bouchard, the French officer in charge of the works, was having his men dig foundations when a spade struck something black and hard. He saw at once that this was no common stone, reported it to his commander, and the commander sent it to the Institut d'Égypte that Napoleon had set up in Cairo, for the scholars to study.

How big is the stone? It stands 112.3 centimeters high (about 44 inches), is 75.7 centimeters wide (about 30 inches) and 28.4 centimeters thick (about 11 inches), and weighs some 762 kilograms (roughly 1,680 pounds) — an adult can just about get his arms around it, and two men can carry it off without much trouble. It was originally part of a larger stele; what remains is a fragment, with a good deal missing at the corners. From top to bottom, its face carries three texts: at the top, 14 lines of ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs; in the middle, 32 lines of Demotic Egyptian; at the bottom, 54 lines of ancient Greek, the ends of many lines broken away.

The stele itself was not rare. As JSTOR Daily puts it, it was a decree stele of the priests, set up in 196 BC in the reign of Ptolemy V, and identical stelae stood in many places across Egypt at the time. Their content was tax reductions and pardons — political propaganda, in plain terms, meant to let people everywhere see the new king's benevolence.

What truly set it apart was the alignment of the three scripts. One and the same decree, carved once in Egyptian hieroglyphs, once in Demotic Egyptian, and once in ancient Greek. Ancient Greek was still a readable language at the end of the eighteenth century, and that amounted to a ready-made key: match the meaning of the Greek, and the other two scripts had a base text to be checked against.

The stone did not stay in French hands for long. In 1801, Britain and France signed the Treaty of Alexandria, and the defeated French army handed the stone over to Britain as spoils of war. There is an episode here: as the French withdrew, unwilling to give the stone up, they once hid it aboard a small boat, meaning to smuggle it back to Europe, but the British intercepted it on the way. In the end the two sides agreed that the French could keep their research and their copies, while the stone itself went to Britain. From 1802 onward it has stood in the British Museum, on display to this day. Inscriptions added by later hands are carved on its sides — on the left, "Captured in Egypt by the British Army in 1801"; on the right, "Presented by King George III" — and they were deliberately preserved when the stone was cleaned.

In its two hundred years and more in the British Museum, the stone has left only once. In 1917, as the First World War drew toward its end and London faced air raids, the museum, fearing the stone might be destroyed in the bombing, hid it together with a batch of important artifacts in a Tube station fifteen meters underground (about fifty feet), where it stayed for two years. Only after the war was over was it carried back to its gallery.

II. Three Scripts, One Decree

To understand the weight of this stone, you first have to know how the hieroglyphs were lost. Ancient Egypt used hieroglyphic writing for more than three thousand years; by the period of Roman rule, fewer and fewer people could write it. The last known hieroglyphic inscription was carved in the Temple of Isis on the island of Philae, in the year 394. After that, no one could read the script at all, and it slept for more than 1,400 years.

The European scholars of the late eighteenth century stood helpless before the hieroglyphs on temple walls. Some held that they were pictures, each sign standing for an idea; others held that they were letters, which could be spelled together into sounds. The two views were argued over for a long time, and neither could persuade the other, because there was no sample to check them against.

The Rosetta Stone was that sample. The three scripts record one and the same decree; the Greek could be read, and what remained was the work of comparison. On the stone, the king's name was enclosed in an oval frame, called a cartouche. By matching the king's name in the Greek, one could pin down the corresponding group of signs in the hieroglyphs.

III. Fourteen Years of Deadlock

The decipherment did not happen at a stroke. After the stone was found, the scholars of Europe worked on it for more than twenty years. The first breakthrough came from the French linguist Silvestre de Sacy and his Swedish student Johan David Åkerblad, who in 1802 explained the inscription by a phonetic theory and successfully read out the names of several kings and queens. The English polymath Thomas Young took up the work in 1814. He first recognized that the signs inside the cartouche spelled "Ptolemy," and that these signs served a phonetic purpose — they were not simply pictures. That step was crucial, but Young stopped there and could push no further.

The French scholar Jean-François Champollion took the baton from him. He was younger than Young and knew more languages — above all Coptic, the descendant of ancient Egyptian. He did not study the Rosetta Stone alone; he brought in the inscriptions on other stelae and on papyri for comparison, and gradually worked out a set of rules: hieroglyphic writing contained both signs that stood for ideas and signs that stood for sounds, the two uses mixed together. He was the first to confirm this theory, and he deciphered the writing of the pharaohs' names "Thutmose" and "Ramesses" — the key clue that unlocked all hieroglyphic writing.

On September 27, 1822, Champollion delivered his report at the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres in Paris, announcing that he had deciphered the hieroglyphs. Of that day, the most widely circulated account says that Champollion rushed into his brother's office, cried "I've found it!", and fainted dead away on the spot. This detail comes from later biographies, not from any record made at the time; in writing it down, one can only say "so the story goes."

There is one more detail, often overlooked: Champollion never in his life saw the original Rosetta Stone with his own eyes. He relied entirely on rubbings and copies. What truly made his decipherment succeed was not that he touched the stone, but that he understood the relationships between languages.

IV. A Sleeping Script Speaks Again

Champollion's success opened a door for Egyptology. Before it, Europeans standing before the pyramids and temples could only watch the spectacle, unable to read what was written on the walls. After it, the liturgies of the temples, the curses in the tombs, and the chronicles of the kings were read out one after another. A civilization that had slept for 1,400 years began to speak again.

The stone's influence long ago spread beyond archaeology. In the nineteenth century an Egypt craze swept Europe, and architecture, furniture, and jewelry all took on Egyptian styles; a century later, the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb set off another wave of it. Today the name "Rosetta Stone" is used by a space probe and by language-learning software alike; it has become a byword for "the key that unlocks a puzzle."

But the fate of the stone itself has to this day no answer that satisfies everyone. Egypt has continually demanded that the British Museum return it, and the former antiquities minister Zahi Hawass and others have called publicly for its return many times. The British Museum's position is that the stone is held lawfully, and that in London more people can see it. The argument continues to this day, and the stone still stands in its London gallery.

V. The Two Faces of One Stone

Looking back, the story of the Rosetta Stone has two faces. One is the scholarly face: three scripts aligned, and one key opening an entire discipline. The other is the historical face: this stone was a piece of propaganda for the Ptolemaic dynasty, a spoil of war dug up by Napoleon's expeditionary army, and a witness to the colonial era's scramble for antiquities. It helped mankind to read the past, and it also reminds people how the past was carried from place to place.

The credit due to Champollion and Thomas Young is being reassessed by recent research as well. Young first recognized the phonetic function of the cartouche; Champollion went further. One Englishman and one Frenchman, rivals to each other, produced between them what amounts to a single relay. Without Young's first half of the course, there would have been no second half from Champollion.

Epilogue: One Stone Rewrites a Discipline

A black stone dug up while building a fort, a decree of tax reductions from 196 BC, more than twenty years of comparison, and one cry of "I've found it." The writing of ancient Egypt slept for 1,400 years, and it took only twenty-odd years for it to speak again.

The lesson in this is a practical one: a puzzle that cannot be solved is usually short not of clever people, but of a sample to check against. The Rosetta Stone was that sample. It does not speak — but it let a dead script begin to speak again.

As an aside, the decree on the stone is itself nothing remarkable. Ptolemy V had been on the throne for one year, and the priests sang his praises, writing of his tax cuts, his pardons, and the statues he set up in the temples. Political propaganda from more than two thousand years ago looks perfectly ordinary today. Who would have thought that a piece of flattering official prose would end up as one of the most important archaeological discoveries in human history? History is sometimes like this: the things the people of the time meant to leave behind did not survive, and the things they left behind by accident became treasures instead.
